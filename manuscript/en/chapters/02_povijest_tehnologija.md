# 2 History and evolution of communication technologies


## 2.1 Communication as the basis and material of civilization

Consideration of the essence of civilization inevitably leads us to its fundamental beginning and material: communication. Civilization can be understood as a dynamic and continuous process of social life, which is entirely enabled, shaped and maintained by communication. Man's nature as a social being dictates the need for the exchange of thoughts, feelings and experiences, and this exchange, mediated by complex symbolic systems, becomes the foundation on which increasingly complex social structures are built.

Communication is therefore a process by which a shared world of meaning is created and maintained. It is the ability to create and transmit abstract ideas and shared narratives – myths, laws, values ​​and history – that enables the cooperation of a large number of individuals and thus lays the cornerstone of any civilization (Harari, 2011). Without this ability to transcend the immediate, experiential world, human communities would remain limited to small, kinship-related groups. Through language, writing, art and other symbolic forms, thought is materialized; it becomes external and accessible to others, regardless of spatial and temporal distance.

This process of materializing thoughts through communication gives birth to what we call *artifacts*. Artifacts in this sense do not only refer to material objects such as tools, buildings or works of art, but also include immaterial creations: laws, social norms, scientific theories, philosophical systems and religious doctrines. Each such artifact is an embodiment of knowledge and experience, a frozen communication waiting to be unraveled and reactivated in the minds of new generations. In this way culture acquires its cumulative nature; each generation does not start anew, but interacts with the inherited information environment shaped by its predecessors.

> **Artifact:** In the context of communication and culture – a material or immaterial creation (law, norm, theory, work of art, technology) in which knowledge and experience are preserved; it mediates between agents across time and space and enables the cumulability of culture.
> **Cumulative culture:** The property of human culture that knowledge, skills and institutions are gradually upgraded from generation to generation, because through communication and artifacts, previous achievements are preserved on which each new generation can build on its own innovations; a mechanism that makes cultural evolution progressive rather than cyclical (Tomasello, 1999; Henrich, 2016).

Civilization can therefore be understood as a complex and inexhaustible interaction network consisting of *agents* (human beings) and *artifacts*. Within that network, communication is the driving force behind all interactions. Agents communicate with each other to coordinate their actions, but they also communicate with artifacts - they read books, interpret laws, use technology, apply scientific knowledge. Artifacts, on the other hand, mediate communication between agents, often across vast temporal and spatial ranges, and thus shape their thinking and actions (Floridi, 2014).

Communication is the material and fabric of civilization itself. It is the process by which abstract ideas can be transformed into permanent artifacts, by which knowledge is transmitted and multiplied, and by which individual agents are connected into a complex, self-sustaining whole that we call civilization. Observing the history of civilization means following the traces of this continuous communication - from the first oral traditions and paintings in caves to today's global digital networks. In every form, communication remains a fundamental prerequisite and an inexhaustible source of human creativity.

## 2.2 Oral tradition and living archives

The term **oral** tradition encompasses a complex process that cannot be reduced to mere memory or simple storage of information about the past. On the contrary, it is an active act of transmitting, shaping and constantly reviving cultural knowledge.

> **Oral transmission:** Way of transmitting knowledge, narrative and culture through speech and memory, without writing; knowledge lives in carriers (living archives) and changes in every performance; basic communication technology before the advent of writing. Cultures based on orality have a fundamentally different attitude towards the past than those based on writing; in them, the past is not a fixed and unchanging whole, but a fluid and adaptable reality that is reaffirmed in every act of storytelling (Ong, 1982). This reality is not preserved in dead letters, but in living people.

The bearers of such tradition can rightly be called **living** archives. However, this name does not imply that they are only passive containers of information, similar to shelves in a library.

> **Living archive:** Bearer of oral tradition who actively interprets, adapts and transmits cultural knowledge; knowledge is embodied in voice, gesture and memory, not in written record; each performance is unique. Their role is active and creative. A living archive interprets, adapts and transmits inherited knowledge, imbuing it with its own experience and understanding of the world. Each of his performances, whether it is a song, a story or a ritual formula, is a unique and unrepeatable act of creation, and not a mere reproduction of an existing template (Lord, 2000).

In this process, the **agent**, that is, the bearer and performer of the tradition, is a key figure who actively shapes the narrative. His role goes beyond the function of a transmitter because through his performance he confirms, and sometimes reshapes, the values ​​and knowledge of the community. The knowledge that the living archive possesses is not abstract, but deeply embodied. It resides in his or her voice, rhythm of speech, gesticulation and memory of the body, making the whole of the performer and the performance inseparable.

The difference between oral and written archives is therefore fundamental. By its very nature, the written archive tends towards staticity, immutability and apparent objectivity, separating knowledge from its human source. On the other hand, the oral archive is inextricably linked to the person of the performer and the social context. It is dynamic, changeable and necessarily imbued with subjectivity, because its meaning is constituted precisely in the living relationship between the narrator, the listener and the moment of performance (Goody & Watt, 1968).

This gives the oral tradition an exceptional social function. It is a cohesive force that creates and sustains a community, providing it with a sense of continuity and belonging through a shared repertoire of stories, beliefs and memories. This is why the content of the delivery is never more important than the context of its performance. Circumstances about the identity of the speaker, to whom he addresses, when and on what occasion he speaks, are an integral part of the message being transmitted. In modern times, we are faced with the paradox of the technological record of oral tradition. While recording enables its preservation in the face of the threat of oblivion, it simultaneously fixes and deadens what is essentially alive, fluid and performative. Such a record, although necessary for the preservation of the artifacts themselves, is only a faint reflection of the completeness of the event of oral creation.

### 2.2.1 The complexity of the oral tradition

The approach to oral tradition is often burdened by a simplified view that reduces it to a mere transmission of unchanging contents from generation to generation, almost as a kind of mechanical memorization of a static repertoire of stories or songs. However, such an understanding ignores its inherent layering and dynamism of living and complex systems of communication, knowledge and art, the nature of which cannot be exhausted by one-sided explanations. Oral traditions represent complete cognitive and social worlds, woven into the very fabric of the community that creates, transmits and constantly reshapes them.

The central element of this complexity is the fact that the oral tradition is not realized in a fixed, written text but in the *performance event*. Each performance represents a unique act of creation in which the agent, that is, the performer (singer, storyteller), does not mechanically reproduce a memorized template, but actively composes it at the moment of presentation (Lord, 1960). In doing so, he makes use of the rich heritage of traditional tools: established formulas, metric patterns, typical scenes and thematic sets. These elements function as building blocks that enable improvisation within the given stylistic and content frameworks. The agent is a co-creator who adapts the tradition to the specific context, audience and own inspiration.

Furthermore, the complexity of the oral tradition is manifested in its *multimodality*. It is rarely reduced exclusively to the verbal component. Sound, rhythm, melody, gestures, facial expressions and the entire physicality of the performer are inextricably intertwined with the spoken or sung word. The meaning is conveyed by the semantics of the sentences, but also by the way they are delivered - by intonation, pauses, accents and emotional charge. The role of the audience is not passive; with her reactions, incentives or silence, she directly participates in shaping the performance, creating a dynamic feedback loop with the performer. Performance is therefore always a dialogic and social act, deeply conditioned by the situation in which it occurs.

> **Multimodality:** Simultaneous use and interweaving of multiple communication channels – speech, gesture, mimicry, intonation, rhythm, music and spatial relationship – in order to convey meaning; in the oral tradition it means the inseparability of the verbal and physical dimensions of performance, while in modern digital communication it includes text, image, sound and video as integrated components of the message.

Finally, a fundamental feature of oral tradition is the creative tension between permanence and changeability. In order to survive, a tradition must possess a sufficient measure of stability that ensures its recognition and continuity through generations. At the same time, in order to remain alive and relevant, it must constantly adapt and change with each new performance. This apparent paradox is resolved in the understanding that traditionality does not lie in the immutability of words, but in the deeper structures and meaning potentials they evoke. Each formula or theme carries with it a wealth of associations and references to the entire tradition, creating what John Miles Foley (1991) calls *traditional referentiality*. Words in oral performance have a far greater semantic charge because they echo the unspoken but present context of the entire traditional knowledge. Because of all of the above, the oral tradition should be viewed as an intricate ecosystem in which the cognitive processes of memory and creation, the social dynamics of performance, and the aesthetic values ​​of language, music, and movement merge into a unique and unrepeatable human experience.

### 2.2.2. Collective imagination and identity
The foundation of human culture and, consequently, collective identity rests on the unique cognitive ability of humans for *common intentionality* – the ability to create and share complex social realities that exist exclusively in the domain of collective imagination. It is this ability, as pointed out by researchers such as Tomasello (2014) and Harari (2014), that distinguishes human culture from the rudimentary forms of social learning present in other primates. While animal communities are based on direct interaction and genetically determined behaviors, human societies are built on shared beliefs in concepts such as nations, money, laws, or human rights, which have no tangible existence outside of shared agreement and imagination.
The most obvious and powerful example of such an imagined reality is the nation, which Benedict Anderson (1983) precisely defined as an *imagined community*. Members of one nation will never know each other, but in their minds there is a vivid idea of ​​community. This sense of belonging does not come from direct contact, but is nourished and maintained through shared symbols such as flags and anthems, collective rituals such as national holidays, and narratives about shared origins, history and destiny. Through these mechanisms, a deep emotional bond is created that connects millions of strangers into a coherent whole.
In the modern world, the reach and forms of the collective imagination have been significantly expanded by the action of the global media. In his analysis of global cultural flows, Arjun Appadurai (1996) introduces the concepts of *mediascapes* and *ideoscapes*. These landscapes, made up of images, narratives and ideas that circulate through the media, enable the creation of identities that transcend traditional national boundaries. Individuals today can develop a sense of belonging to global subcultures, social movements or diasporic communities, thus confirming that the collective imagination is not limited exclusively to the territory of the nation-state.
However, it is important to emphasize that the collective imagination also has a distinct material dimension. Imagined realities are embodied and made permanent through monuments, architecture, flags, legal documents and social institutions. These material objects and structures act as *anchors* of collective identity, giving abstract ideas a concrete and visible form (Latour, 2005). The monument to the unknown hero is not only a stone but also a materialized narrative of sacrifice and national pride; the constitution is not only a piece of paper but also a tangible promise of common rights and order.
At the same time, the field of collective imagination is dynamic and fraught with conflicts. Different social groups are constantly competing to impose their own vision of reality and define dominant narratives about who "we" are. Identity is therefore not a static and predetermined category, but, as Judith Butler (Butler and Trouble 1990) shows in the context of gender, is the result of a continuous process of negotiation, contestation and performative repetition. History is filled with struggles over the interpretation of the past, the meaning of national symbols or the definition of community boundaries, which testifies to the fact that collective identity is always an open arena of power.
In this complex power relationship, the individual agent actively participates in the process of creating meaning - he interprets, accepts, adapts, but also resists or reshapes collective ideas. At this intersection of individual action and collective structures, the real life of identity takes place. Through the action of the agent, the dominant imagination can be further strengthened and confirmed, but it can also be contested, undermined and finally changed, thereby opening up space for the creation of new forms of collective existence.

### 2.2.3 Social functions of oral tradition
To view the oral tradition exclusively as a treasury of antiquities or a mere repository of aesthetically shaped texts would be to overlook its fundamental purpose and dynamic role in community life. Its existence is certainly conditioned by the individual need for artistic expression, but above all by its deep and multi-layered rootedness in social reality. Oral traditions, in all their forms - from myths and epics to proverbs and songs - act as key mechanisms through which a community designs, maintains and transmits its entire worldview and social structure. They become an active *agent* in its shaping.
One of the most important roles of oral tradition is the preservation and transmission of **collective** memory. In cultures that primarily rely on orality, the stories about the origin of the community, about the deeds of the ancestors, about mythical events and historical milestones form the living fabric of historical consciousness. As a narrative framework that gives meaning and legitimacy to the present, oral tradition connects the community to its origins and ensures a sense of continuity through generations, and establishes and maintains a common identity, because belonging to a community means sharing and knowing its stories.

> **Collective memory:** A socially conditioned and jointly maintained system of memory by which the group preserves, interprets and transmits selected ideas about its own past; in the oral tradition, it is realized through repeated narrative performances (myths, genealogies, epic poems) that give meaning and legitimacy to the present and ensure a sense of continuity and belonging across generations (Halbwachs, 1950; Assmann, 2011).

Furthermore, oral tradition is a fundamental means of education and **socialization**. Through short stories, fairy tales, riddles and songs, the young generations adopt primarily the language and aesthetic patterns of their culture, but also ethical codes, social norms, rules of conduct and practical knowledge necessary for survival. Instructive stories warn of the consequences of transgression, while heroic poems offer models of desirable action. Thus, tradition acts as an informal but extremely effective educational system that shapes an individual into a full-fledged and functional member of society.

> **Socialization:** The process by which an individual, in interaction with other members of the community, adopts the language, values, norms, knowledge and patterns of behavior necessary for functioning within a certain culture; in oral societies, this process takes place predominantly through narrative and performance forms (stories, fairy tales, riddles, songs) that simultaneously teach, entertain and shape personal and social identity (Berger and Luckmann, 1966).

Oral traditions also have a distinct **pragmatic and legal-legitimation function**. Myths about the creation of the world or the origin of tribes often serve as charters that confirm and justify existing social order, property relations, political hierarchy, or ritual practices (Finnegan, 1977). Genealogies that are remembered and publicly recited legitimize the right of ruling lineages to power, while oral traditions about court precedents can have the force of unwritten law. Oral tradition thus provides the basis for social stability, offering a supernatural or historical sanction for secular institutions.

> **Pragmatic function of oral tradition:** The role of oral traditions in the direct regulation of social life - from the legal legitimation of government and property relations (through genealogies and myths of origin) to the transfer of practical knowledge and skills necessary for daily survival; tradition acts as an unwritten law and operational manual of the community (Finnegan, 1977; Malinowski, 1926).

The **fun** and aesthetic function, which is inseparable from all the others, should not be neglected either. Oral performances provide aesthetic pleasure and entertainment, gathering the community in moments of rest and celebration. The skill of the storyteller, singer, or reciter—their ability to master voice, gesture, and interaction with the audience—is critical to the success of message transmission (Bauman, 1986). It is precisely through the artistic dimension of the performance that the content of the tradition becomes attractive, memorable and emotionally effective.

> **Aesthetic function:** The dimension of the communication act in which the form of the message – rhythm, melody, figure, style, narrative structure – becomes a source of pleasure and meaning in itself; in the oral tradition, the aesthetic function is not just a decoration, but a key mechanism that makes the content memorable, emotionally effective and suitable for repeated performance, thus directly ensuring the transmission of culture (Jakobson, 1960; Bauman, 1986).

Finally, oral tradition can also serve as a form of social **criticism** and commentary, and even as a covert vent for expressing dissent. Through satirical songs, anecdotes about powerful people or allegorical stories about animals, the community can criticize injustice, mock authority or question ruling norms in a way that is often too risky in direct communication. Thus, tradition shows its adaptability and ability to serve not only the maintenance, but also the questioning and subtle change of the social order.

> **Social criticism in the oral tradition:** The function of the oral tradition that allows the community to question, comment on and challenge the existing power relations and social norms through indirect narrative forms – satire, allegory, parody, anecdotes about the powerful and animal fables; since criticism comes in an aesthetically shaped and collectively sanctioned form, it bypasses open conflict while simultaneously opening up space for reconsideration and gradual change of order (Scott, 1990; Finnegan, 1977).

All the mentioned functions – from preservation of memory and education to identification and entertainment – ​​are intertwined and can rarely be viewed separately. Each individual oral performance simultaneously activates several of these roles, confirming that oral tradition is a complete social phenomenon, necessary for the survival and cohesion of the community that creates and nurtures it.
### 2.3 Literacy as a window into complex knowledge systems
Observing literacy exclusively as the skill of reading and writing means overlooking its deep, transformative role in shaping human thought and the entire social reality. Literacy should be understood as a fundamental technology that changes the cognitive architecture of the individual and opens access to complex, cumulative systems of knowledge, unimaginable in exclusively oral cultures. It is an epistemological prerequisite for the emergence of science, philosophy, law and history in their modern form.
The main dividing line that literacy establishes in relation to oral tradition is its ability to externalize and fix thoughts. While the spoken word is inherently transitory and tied to the immediate context of interaction, the written text exists as an autonomous and permanent object, separate from the author and the moment of creation. This durability enables new cognitive operations: re-reading, careful analysis, comparison of different statements created in different times and places, and systematic criticism (Goody & Watt, 1963). The text thereby establishes itself as an external, objectified memory, freeing the mind from the cognitive burden of remembering the entire cultural heritage and allowing it to devote itself to abstract and analytical thinking.

> **Externalization of thinking:** The process of transferring internal cognitive content - ideas, knowledge, reasoning - to an external, permanent and author-independent carrier (written text, diagram, record, digital document); thus, the mind is relieved of the burden of memory, and thought becomes available for re-reading, analysis, criticism and cumulative upgrading through generations, thus opening the prerequisites for the emergence of complex systems of knowledge (Goody, 1977; Donald, 1991; Clark and Chalmers, 1998).

This change profoundly reshapes internal mental processes as well. Mastery of writing encourages the development of linear, sequential thinking, as opposed to the associative and episodic nature of memory in oral cultures. The ability to follow complex syntax, classify information into categories and subcategories, and develop abstract concepts is a direct consequence of long-term exposure to the written word. In addition to storing knowledge, literacy literally restructures neural pathways in the brain, creating prerequisites for new forms of intelligence and introspection (Dehaene, 2009).
It is on these cognitive foundations that complex systems of knowledge grow. Science, for example, rests on the possibility of verifying and upgrading previously recorded knowledge; legal systems depend on the precision and immutability of written laws; and philosophical and theological discourses develop through centuries of written debates about the foundational texts. These systems have an internal logic, hierarchy, and cumulative nature, which would be impossible to maintain solely through word of mouth. Written culture creates a vast, intertextual network into which every new knowledge must fit, to which it must refer and which gives it additional meaning.
In this complex universe of knowledge, a literate individual becomes an *agent* - an employee who, through literacy, acquires the means to understand existing systems, as well as to actively participate in them. He can interpret, question, challenge and, finally, contribute to the body of knowledge. Through the act of reading, the agent enters into a dialogue with thinkers from the past, and through the act of writing, he articulates his own knowledge and directs it to future generations (Ong, 1982).

### 2.3.1 Different paths to the letter
The appearance of writing in human history can be understood as a layered process that took place in different ways, at different times and prompted by different needs. Historical and archeological findings bear witness to at least four independent, spontaneous centers of literacy: in Mesopotamia, Egypt, China and Central America. Each of these systems emerged from specific cultural and social circumstances, but the paths that letters and literacy moved on branched off into several fundamental directions.
The fundamental difference that emerges in the study of the history of letters is that between the original invention, i.e. the creation of letters *de novo*, and the process of borrowing or adapting an already existing system. The creation of a letter from the ground up is an extremely rare historical phenomenon, related to the mentioned origins. Much more common is the path of adaptation, where one culture takes a script from another and modifies it to fit the structure of its language. It is a paradigmatic example of the Greek takeover of the Phoenician consonant letter (abjada). The Greeks, faced with a script that did not record the vowels crucial to the phonology of their Indo-European language, repurposed graphemes for the Semitic gutturals they did not need and assigned them the value of vowels. This only resulted in the transmission of an essential structural change that resulted in the first complete alphabet. Similar processes of adaptation have occurred countless times in history, each time confirming that the script is a dynamic system that must adapt to the phonological, morphological and syntactic features of the language it serves.
Furthermore, the path to a well-formed writing system can also be viewed through the prism of the way it was created, which can be gradual and emergent, or conscious and engineered.
**The emergent or organic path implies a long-term,** often century-long development, during which the alphabet gradually develops from simpler protosystems, such as pictograms, ideograms or systems for recording numbers and goods. The most famous example is the Sumerian cuneiform script, which evolved from a system of clay tokens used for administrative and economic purposes millennia before the appearance of real writing (Schmandt-Besserat, 1996). This development was a response to the growing needs of a complex society for accurate records. In such a model, the letter appears as the result of a collective and unconscious process, without a named inventor or a precise moment of creation.

*Figure 2.5: Development paths of the alphabet (from pictograph to alphabet).*
**The engineered or deliberate path is the opposite.** Here, writing is the result of a conscious and deliberate act of creation undertaken by an individual or a small group. Such an agent acts with a clear goal - to create a script for a specific language, often for cultural, political or religious reasons. The most obvious example of such an endeavor is the Korean script Hangul, which was devised in the 15th century by King Sejong the Great and his scholars to increase literacy and strengthen Korean cultural identity. Hangul was not created by adapting an existing script, but was designed based on phonological analysis of the Korean language, making it one of the most scientifically sound scripts in the world. Similar, although less well-known, examples are the Cherokee language script created in the 19th century by Sequoyah or the numerous scripts designed by Christian missionaries for previously unwritten languages ​​(Cromer, 1997).
These two dichotomies – *de novo* versus adaptation and emergent versus engineering – are not always strictly separate and can be intertwined. The adaptation of an existing script can also be the result of a deliberate act, such as contemporary orthographic reforms, or it can occur gradually and organically over centuries of use.

Understanding these different paths is crucial because it shows that there is no single "evolution" of writing that necessarily leads from pictorial to alphabetic. Each writing system is a reflection of the history, culture and linguistic structure of the community that uses it. The life of the letter is inseparably connected with the life of that community, and the ways in which it develops and spreads are as diverse as human societies themselves.
### 2.3.2. Social elite and slow expansion
In the complex fabric of social relations, the social elite occupies a dual and sometimes contradictory role in the processes of innovation diffusion. Although history has recorded elites as the first adopters of certain innovations, especially those that strengthen their status, their position in the power structure often makes them a key factor in slowing down or even stopping diffusion processes. This phenomenon often results from active action aimed at preserving the existing order (*status quo*).
The fundamental cause of such action lies in the fact that every major innovation, whether technological, cultural or social in nature, has the potential to redistribute power, influence and resources within the community. The social elite, by definition, benefits the most from the existing situation. Their power and privileged position are directly linked to existing norms, values ​​and material relations. Therefore, any innovation that could disturb this balance is perceived as a threat. Preservation of their own interests thus becomes the driving force of resistance to changes that come from outside their controlled circles.

> **Redistribution of power:** A change in the distribution of influence, resources and authority among actors within the social structure, which occurs when a new technology, practice or idea changes the previous channels of access to knowledge, communication or material means; in the context of communication innovations (letters, press, internet) the redistribution of power means the process by which previously excluded groups gain the ability to act, while the previous elites lose their monopoly over information and interpretation of reality (Castells, 2009; Eisenstein, 1979).

Furthermore, social stratification itself creates barriers to expansion. Social elites are often characterized by a high degree of homophily - a tendency to associate and interact with individuals who are similar in education, background and status. Such confinement to one's own social networks significantly limits the flow of information and influence between the elite and the wider population. Communication channels become interrupted or one-way, and the elite acts as a kind of "gatekeeper", filtering which ideas and practices will reach the rest of society. As Rogers (2003) states, when elites adopt an innovation exclusively for themselves to emphasize their special status, they actually prevent its further diffusion, instead of encouraging it.

> **Social stratification:** Hierarchical stratification of society into levels (layers, classes, estates) that differ in access to material resources, education, power and prestige; in the context of communication technologies, stratification determines who first adopts an innovation, who has access to information and who acts as a gatekeeper that accelerates or slows down the spread of new ideas and practices to other parts of society (Weber, 1922; Bourdieu, 1984; Rogers, 2003).

The role of a change agent in such a context is extremely difficult. If the agent does not belong to the elite or does not have direct access to its members, his messages will have difficulty overcoming social and cultural distance. The elite may reject an innovation not so much because of its intrinsic properties as because of the origin of its bearer. News that comes from "below" or from the outside can be perceived as vulgar, inappropriate or threatening, thereby discrediting its symbolic value in advance. This establishes a mechanism in which the elite not only slows down change, but actively works to delegitimize innovations that do not serve to strengthen its position.

> **Cultural distance:** Degree of difference in values, norms, language, education and life experiences between two social groups; the greater the cultural distance between the bearer of the innovation and potential recipients, the more difficult is the transfer of ideas and practices, because the message loses credibility or is reinterpreted through the prism of the recipient's own symbolic framework (Rogers, 2003; Hofstede, 2001).

## 2.4 The printing press: a turning point in the spread of ideas

The invention of the movable type printing press, which is attributed to Johannes Gutenberg around 1450, represents one of the most significant technological and cultural breakthroughs in human history. Before Gutenberg, knowledge was transmitted and preserved in the form of manuscripts, the creation of which was a long, painstaking and extremely expensive process, mostly limited to monastery scriptoriums and court workshops. Each copy was unique, and its creation could take months, even years, which made the book a rare and precious object available only to a small elite. The limited number of copies and the high price significantly slowed down and narrowed the flow of information and ideas.
Gutenberg's invention fundamentally transformed the material reality of the book and thereby triggered an avalanche of social, religious and scientific changes. By combining the technology of movable metal letters, a press adapted from winemaking, and newly developed oil-based printing ink, a system was created that enabled fast, cheap and mass production of texts. While the monk-copyist wrote a single manuscript, the printing press could now reproduce hundreds and thousands of identical copies. This change in the scale of production necessarily led to a drastic drop in price, with which the written word began its journey towards wider layers of society, gradually leaving the exclusive space of church and noble libraries.
The consequences of that technological revolution were far-reaching and profound. The printing press became a key *agent* in spreading the ideas of the Reformation. The ability to quickly print and distribute writings, such as Martin Luther's *95 Theses*, allowed criticism of church practices to spread across Europe at a speed previously unimaginable. This broke the monopoly of the Catholic Church over the interpretation of holy scriptures and religious doctrines. The printing of the Bible in the vernacular languages ​​further strengthened this process, allowing individuals direct access to the text, which until then had been mediated exclusively by the clergy in the Latin language.
In the field of science, the press enabled what is today considered the basis of scientific progress: the reliable and wide dissemination of knowledge. Scientists could more easily share their discoveries, and the accuracy of printed texts and diagrams, as opposed to manuscripts subject to copying errors, provided a solid foundation for further research. As Elizabeth Eisenstein (1979) points out in her capital work, this "fixity of print" created the preconditions for the cumulative growth of knowledge and the scientific revolution that followed in the following centuries. The ideas of Copernicus, Galileo or Newton could circulate among scholars across Europe, encouraging debate, verification and improvement.

> **Dissemination of knowledge:** Systematic dissemination of knowledge, ideas and information from the source to a wider circle of recipients through communication channels (manuscript, print, digital network); is a key assumption of cumulative culture because it allows discoveries to become available for verification, critique, and upgrade by other agents, thus transforming knowledge from an individual achievement into a common good that accelerates further progress (Eisenstein, 1979; Rogers, 2003).

*Figure 2.8: The impact of the printing press on the spread of knowledge and social changes: from Gutenberg's invention (c. 1440) and the mass production of written materials to the increased availability of books, the spread of literacy and ideas (humanism, the Renaissance, the Reformation), the standardization of language and the emergence of the public sphere and public opinion.*

Furthermore, the press played a crucial role in the formation of modern nations and the standardization of vernacular languages. Printers, in search of a wider market, chose and promoted certain dialects, which, thanks to the mass distribution of printed works, gradually imposed themselves as the language standard. This created the basis for linguistic and cultural unity in wider areas, which was one of the cornerstones in the process of creating national identities.
Viewed in this light, the printing press is an active *agent* of change which, according to the insights of Marshall McLuhan (1962), reshaped human sensorium and cognitive processes. By emphasizing the visual over the auditory, the press encouraged the development of a linear, sequential and analytical way of thinking, which became a feature of Western civilization in the period known as "Gutenberg's Galaxy". The influence of the printing press is therefore not exhausted in technical innovation; he reshaped the ways in which people think, communicate and organize their societies, laying the foundations for the emergence of the modern world.

### 2.4.1 Mass production and democratization of knowledge
The appearance of mass production of books, made possible by Gutenberg's invention of the movable type printing press in the middle of the 15th century, should be considered a turning point in the history of Western civilization. Until that time, knowledge contained in books was confined within narrow circles. Books were rare and precious items, multiplied by painstaking work of copyists in monastery scriptoriums, which made them available almost exclusively to the priestly and noble elite. The printing press fundamentally changed that order, enabling the rapid and significantly cheaper production of printed materials and creating the prerequisites for a more extensive spread of literacy among the wider strata of the population.
This transformation was particularly clearly manifested during the Protestant Reformation. Martin Luther, recognizing the power of the new media, used the press for the mass dissemination of his writings, thereby inciting religious and social changes of unprecedented proportions. Equally, the press served as a powerful stimulus for the development of science. It enabled scholars to exchange knowledge and discoveries with a previously unimaginable speed, which laid the foundations for the scientific revolution and the establishment of a systematic scientific discourse (Eisenstein, 1979). A kind of *Res publica litterarum* was created, a community of scholars all over Europe connected by the printed word, who could discuss, build on and check the works of their contemporaries.
This entire process is rightly called the **democratization** of knowledge. However, this term does not mean only easy access to existing information. Its meaning is deeper and includes the gradual empowerment of individuals for active participation in the creation, verification and dissemination of new knowledge. Knowledge thus ceases to be the exclusive property of privileged groups and becomes a common good, subject to public discussion and criticism.

> **Democratization of knowledge:** Gradual removal of social, economic and institutional obstacles to access to information and knowledge, which gives an ever-widening circle of agents the ability not only to receive but also to create, verify and disseminate knowledge; historically initiated by the invention of printing, and nowadays accelerated by digital technology and open access, the democratization of knowledge transforms it from the privilege of the few into a common good subject to public discussion and criticism (Eisenstein, 1979; Castells, 2010).

![](../../docs/diagrams_en/diag_903.svg)
*Figure 2.10: The path of knowledge from limited access in manuscript culture to wider availability through the printing revolution, culminating in the democratization of knowledge.*

It should, of course, be noted that this transition was neither immediate nor without resistance; it took centuries for the effects of the printing revolution to fully manifest and permeate every pore of society. In today's time, with the advent of digital technology and the Internet, a transformation of comparable, if not greater, proportions is taking place. The Internet can be seen as a contemporary counterpart to the printing press, which accelerates and expands the process of democratization of knowledge to a global level, bringing with it new opportunities, but also new challenges.

### 2.4.2 Reformation and scientific revolution
The period of the 16th and 17th centuries in European history was marked by two profound and transformative movements: the Reformation and the Scientific Revolution. Their simultaneity, as well as their partial geographical overlap, encourage consideration of complex connections and mutual influences. Although a simple cause-and-effect relationship cannot be established, it is clear that the Reformation created an intellectual, social and cultural atmosphere that favored the emergence and development of modern science.
The invention of the printing press played a key role in the spread of reformation ideas, but also in creating the basis for scientific progress. By enabling the mass and rapid reproduction of texts, the press undermined the Catholic Church's monopoly over the knowledge and interpretation of scriptures. The principle of *sola scriptura*, one of the central pillars of the Reformation, encouraged believers to independently read and reflect on the Bible, which consequently led to an increase in literacy. This newly acquired skill and habit of critical reading was not limited exclusively to religious texts, but also spread to other areas of human knowledge, including scientific ones. The availability of printed works enabled a faster and wider exchange of ideas among scholars across Europe, creating the foundations for the scholarly community as we know it today.
In its essence, the Reformation represented a fundamental challenge to the spiritual and secular authority of the Catholic Church. Martin Luther's act, which publicly questioned the infallibility of the pope and church councils, resonated far beyond theological circles. This undermining of a monolithic authority created an intellectual atmosphere in which it became conceivable, and even desirable, to question other established dogmas, including scientific ones, such as the Aristotelian-Ptolemaic geocentric system, which the Church accepted. If the authority of the Church could be questioned in the field of faith, why could it not be questioned in the field of interpretation of nature? In this way, the Reformation, albeit unintentionally, contributed to the secularization of knowledge and made critical questioning legitimate as a valid cognitive method.
The Protestant doctrine of the "priesthood of all believers", which gives each individual the right and duty to independently interpret God's word, finds its scientific analogy. The scientist, as an individual agent, now assumes the role of one who directly "reads" and interprets the "book of nature", without the mediation of ancient authorities such as Aristotle or Galen. This new epistemological paradigm, which emphasized personal experience and interpretation, was not limited to theology. It was also transferred to the study of nature, which began to be perceived as "God's second book" (Harrison, 2007), open to reading and interpretation through observation and experiment. In his famous thesis, Max Weber connected Protestant ethics with the spirit of capitalism, and Robert K. Merton established a similar connection between Puritanism, as one of the branches of Protestantism, and the development of experimental science in England in the 17th century (Merton, 1970). According to Merton, Puritan values ​​such as diligence, methodology, and utilitarianism encouraged scientific research that could bring practical benefit and glorify God through the discovery of the laws of his creation.

![](../../docs/diagrams_en/diag_10.svg)
*Figure 2.11: Schematic representation of the key consequences of the Reformation that contributed to the creation of an enabling environment for the scientific revolution: Reformation incentives, reinforced by printing technology, lead to the fragmentation of authority and the emphasis on individual knowledge, which creates fertile ground for new scientific methods and discoveries.*

However, it should be emphasized that the relationship between the Reformation and science was not uniquely encouraging. The reformers themselves, including Luther and Calvin, expressed considerable skepticism and even open opposition to some new scientific ideas, such as Copernicus' heliocentric theory. Their primary goal was the restoration of faith, not the promotion of science. Furthermore, the Scientific Revolution was not an exclusively Protestant phenomenon; scientists like Copernicus, Galileo and Descartes worked within the Catholic world. Therefore, the impact of the Reformation on science is not so much manifested in direct doctrinal support as in its indirect and often unintentional consequences: the fragmentation of intellectual authority, the promotion of literacy and the culture of reading, and the establishment of a new value system in which the individual agent, either as a believer or as a scientist, became the center of the cognitive process.

## 2.5 Telegraph and telephone: The beginning of electronic communication
The nineteenth century brought a fundamental change in the history of human communication by introducing technologies that for the first time enabled the transmission of messages at a speed greater than any physical vehicle. This marked the beginning of the era of electronic communication, the foundations of which are still felt today in all modern networks.

### 2.5.1 Telegraph: new speeds and new horizons
The electric telegraph profoundly and permanently altered the contours of human communication, establishing a new paradigm that ended the millennia-old dependence of information transmission on the physical speed of the messenger, whether by horseman, stagecoach, or ship. Before the telegraph, the world moved at the speed of muscle and wind. After him, the message could threaten the movement of the Sun from east to west, and the distance became a magnitude that, for the first time in history, could be overcome almost instantly.
The basic principle of the telegraph was based on a simple but ingenious idea: the conversion of electrical impulses, sent through a long metal wire, into coded characters. Morse code, with its system of dots and dashes, became the *lingua franca* of that new age, the abstract language of machines that human operators, skilled *agents* of mediation, turned into meaningful words. This new speed was astonishing. News that would have taken weeks to travel across the Atlantic now reached its destination in minutes. This quantitative change - the drastic reduction of the time required for communication - necessarily stimulated profound qualitative changes in the organization of society.
By accelerating existing communication practices, the telegraph created a qualitative leap that gave birth to completely new forms of social and economic interaction. New horizons of possibilities that were previously unimaginable have opened up. Capital and commodity markets, such as those in London and New York, could operate as a single, synchronized system as prices and transactions became instantly available on both sides of the ocean. The business empire could be coordinated from a single center, and armies were given the possibility of centralized command in real time. News agencies, such as Reuters and the Associated Press, grew up precisely on the telegraph network, creating a global flow of news and reshaping public opinion (Standage, 1998). This changed the very perception of space and time; the world began to seem smaller, more connected and busier.
To view the telegraph exclusively as a technological artifact would be to ignore its role as the cornerstone of the new communication order. It was a complex socio-technical system that included devices and wires, but also international standards, protocols for sending messages and a whole new profession - telegraph operators. These skilled operators, as key human *agents* in the communication chain, were an indispensable part of the functioning of the entire network. Their ability to quickly and accurately encode and decode messages was as important as the correctness of the technical facilities themselves. The entire process can be presented as a sequence of steps in which each element has its own irreplaceable role.
The laying of the first transatlantic telegraph cable in 1866 was a feat comparable to later space exploration – a triumph of human perseverance and engineering skill. This act symbolically, but also realistically, created a unique information space that encompassed the Old and New Worlds. The telegraph thus laid the foundations for all future forms of electronic communication, from telephone and radio to the Internet. It was the first technology to separate communication from transport, proving that a message can move independently of the physical transfer of objects or people (Carey, 1989). In this sense, the telegraph network of the nineteenth century was not only a predecessor, but an archetype of today's global information society, and its echoes still shape the way we live, work and understand the world around us.

### 2.5.2 Telephone: an intimate revolution on the wire
The appearance of the telephone, a technical miracle at the end of the nineteenth century, initiated a further radical transformation of social relations and the very perception of space and time. While the telegraph had already made the world smaller by transmitting coded messages over vast distances, its reach remained in the official, commercial and military spheres. Communication was mediated, asynchronous and devoid of human voice. The telephone, in contrast, returned the most personal thing to the act of communication - the living word, with its color, emphasis, pauses and all those ineffable nuances that reflect human communication. This was the beginning of a quiet but deep revolution that did not take place in the squares or in the parliaments, but in the privacy of homes, on the invisible "wire" that connected the two interlocutors.
The key change introduced by the telephone was the abolition of the necessity of physical presence for immediate and intimate communication. Spatial distance, previously an insurmountable obstacle for maintaining close ties, suddenly became surmountable. The human voice, as a carrier of identity and emotions, traveled for kilometers, creating the illusion of proximity and establishing a unique acoustic space shared by only two interlocutors. In this newly created virtual space, one could whisper, console, argue and confide secrets, making the phone a powerful *agent* in maintaining family and friendship ties that would otherwise weaken under the burden of geographic separation (Fischer, 1992).
The new possibility had far-reaching consequences for the structure of everyday life. In the business world, the telephone accelerated decision-making and the flow of information, but its deepest mark remained in the private sphere. For women, who at that time were often spatially limited to the household, the telephone represented a window to the world. It enabled them to build and maintain their own social networks independently of male family members, organize social events and manage the household in a more efficient way. It became a tool of socialization, but also a symbol of a certain social status. The sound of the telephone ringing in the home has become a sign of connection with the outside world.
However, this connection also had its other side. The immediacy offered by the telephone also meant the possibility of an unannounced intrusion into the privacy of the home. The ringing of the telephone has become a command that demands immediate attention, interrupting family meals, conversations or moments of rest. The border between public and private, once firmly defined by the doorstep, has become permeable. The disembodied nature of the telephone voice, devoid of the visual cues of body language, could also evoke feelings of anxiety or mistrust. The telephone thus established a new dialectic of closeness and distance, availability and threatened privacy, which will become one of the central issues of modern life.
In its essence, the telephone democratized synchronous long-distance communication, transferring it from an exclusively institutional framework to the hands of ordinary people. Its appearance has not only changed the way people talk, but also what they talk about and with whom. It established the foundations for a culture of constant availability and networking, the ultimate consequences of which we will only experience with the advent of mobile and Internet technologies a century later. Therefore, the telephone can rightly be considered the first true "intimate technology" - a device that reshaped the very foundations of human closeness.

## 2.6 Radio and television as catalysts of mass experience
Entering the twentieth century, the concept of space, time and community was reshaped by the appearance of two media that will mark the epoch: radio and television. Their development further initiated a deep transformation of social reality, establishing new forms of collective experience of the world. It was radio and television that became the first true catalysts of mass experience, capable of simultaneously connecting millions of people in the unique act of listening or watching.
The advent of radio in the first decades of the century brought a revolution in sound. For the first time in history, a human voice, music or news of a distant event could overcome physical barriers and enter directly into the privacy of the home. Radio broke the silence and filled everyday life with a new soundscape, but its real power lay in its ability to create a shared imaginative space. Listening to the broadcast of an important political speech, sports match or radio drama, an individual became aware that at the same moment thousands, even millions of strangers were sharing this experience with him. This awareness of simultaneity created a bond that did not exist before, an invisible network that connected the dispersed audience into a single listening body.
In the middle of the century, television supplemented that sound image with the visual power of the moving image, which made its influence even more immediate and comprehensive. The television soon assumed the role of a central place in the home, becoming a "window to the world" through which scenes of coronations, space flights, wars and cultural spectacles entered the living rooms. The power of television rested in its ability to mediate reality seemingly directly and unfiltered. The visual experience of events like the 1969 moon landing did not leave much room for individual interpretation; he offered a ready-made, common image of reality that was engraved in the collective memory. In this way, television has become the ultimate synchronizing mechanism, uniting national and global communities in a shared, visually authenticated experience.
It is precisely this synchronized and collective participation in a media-mediated event that forms the core of the mass experience. It is not exhausted in the fact that a large number of people receive the same information, but in the pervasive awareness of the commonality of that act. This sense of belonging to a temporary but extremely strong community of viewers or listeners strengthened social cohesion and created common reference points in culture. The events broadcast by radio and television became the main topics of conversation, part of the general culture and the basis for building a common identity.
Therefore, the role of radio and television went far beyond the passive transmission of content. They acted as active *agents* of social change. They shaped public opinion, established cultural norms, created heroes and icons, and directed the emotional life of the nation. The way these media framed reality, chose topics and presented events had a decisive influence on the perception of the world and one's own place in it. As media theorists point out, the medium itself, its technological nature and the way it organizes perception becomes a message that reshapes human experience and social relations (McLuhan, 1964). Radio and television did not only report on history; they created it, turning millions of individuals into participants of the same, mass and historical moment.

### 2.6.1 The rise of radio and the "voice" of common reality
The appearance of radio as the first truly mass electronic media fundamentally transformed the social reality of the first half of the twentieth century. Its key feature, which distinguished it from all previous forms of communication, was the ability to create simultaneity of experience on a previously unimaginable scale. For the first time in history, millions of people, spatially separated, could listen to the same content at the same moment, thus becoming participants in a unique, shared sound reality.
This unique sense of collective participation led Marshall McLuhan (1964) to the famous metaphor of the radio as a "tribal drum", an instrument that brings together a dispersed community in a unique rhythm and resonance. Radio thereby became a powerful **agent of social cohesion**, weaving invisible threads that connected individuals in their homes into a large, imagined community of listeners. In times of social crises, wars or major national events, the radio was a focal point that homogenized the national mood and created a sense of destiny connection.
At the center of that new media paradigm was the **human** voice. Freed from the visual context, reduced to its sonic essence, the voice gained exceptional strength and intimacy through radio waves. He was not only a carrier of semantic content, but a powerful medium of emotions, presence and personality. The sound of the voice filling the privacy of the home created the illusion of an immediate, almost personal relationship between the speaker and the millions of individual listeners. This parasocial interaction was key to understanding the impact of radio - the voice became an agent that bridged public and private space, bringing distant events and personalities into the intimate circle of the family hearth.
Statesmen and political leaders soon recognized the power of this new medium for shaping public opinion and mobilizing the masses. Voice has become a fundamental political tool. For example, American President Franklin D. Roosevelt's "Fireside Chats" are a paradigmatic example of using radio to establish trust and leadership. Addressing the nation in a calm and personal tone, Roosevelt managed to create the impression that he was talking to each citizen individually, offering comfort and guidance during the difficult times of the Great Depression. In this context, the voice became a symbol of stability and authority that was both distant and incredibly close at the same time.

His influence permeated the entire cultural life, shaping musical taste, popularizing dramatic forms through radio dramas and creating the first stars of the airwaves. Radio thus established a sonic tapestry of common culture, a frame of reference shared by members of different social strata. It is this ability of radio to simultaneously broadcast a unique "voice" - be it the voice of an announcer, politician, actor or singer - and make it part of the private experience of millions, which has created what we can call "the voice of shared reality". It was a sound reflection and, at the same time, a constitutive element of the consciousness of belonging to a wider social entity.

### 2.6.2. Television and the visual aspect of shared reality
Television should be understood as a key *agent* in the shaping of social reality and collective consciousness, which actively constitutes a common symbolic space within which community life takes place. It is precisely in its visual nature that the basis of its power to contribute to the creation of what can be called a shared imagination lies.
Unlike print media, which largely shaped national consciousness through language and abstract narratives, television offers a direct, visual insight into the world. This ability to *show* events, people and spaces, and not just describe them, gives the imagined community a tangible, concrete form. The notion of "imagined community" by Benedict Anderson (1983), originally linked to the rise of the press and capitalism, acquires a new, stronger dimension in the age of television. Millions of people, who will never meet in person, share the same visual repertoire, the same images of the nation, its successes, crises and rituals through the television screen. This creates a deep sense of belonging based on shared viewing and visual experience.
Furthermore, the power of television does not derive exclusively from its visuality, but also from its ability to create the illusion of simultaneity. The feeling that an event is happening "live" and that the entire community is watching it at the same moment creates a strong social bond. This sense of sharing the present moment bridges the physical distance between individuals and integrates them into a single, synchronized auditory body.
This mechanism is most evident in the phenomenon that Daniel Dayan and Elihu Katz (1992) called "media events". These are extraordinary occasions - such as state ceremonies, major sports competitions, royal weddings or funerals - that interrupt the usual course of programs and life. These events are shown with great respect and ceremony, turning television viewing into a kind of civil rite. By participating in this ritual, viewers not only receive information, but also confirm the fundamental values ​​and norms of their community, thereby renewing and strengthening social cohesion.
Television, therefore, acts as a central mediator in the weaving of the visual and ritual fabric of modern communities. By synthesizing an image that provides evidence and a sense of simultaneity that creates community, it makes the imaginary community visible and present in the daily life of its members.

### 2.6.3 Forerunner of the information society
The emergence of the information society, although it is often associated with the second half of the twentieth century and the accelerated development of digital technology, has its deep roots in the theoretical assumptions and visionary ideas that preceded it. These ideas, formed before there were technical possibilities for their full implementation, created the intellectual foundation for understanding information as a central resource and a key driver of social change. Among the thinkers whose work was decisive, Vannevar Bush and Norbert Wiener stand out, whose concepts permanently shaped the architecture of the contemporary digital world.
Vannevar Bush in his famous essay *As We May Think* (Bush, 1945) presents a vision that prophetically announced the future of knowledge management. The central concept of that essay is *memex*, an imagined device that embodies a completely new way of organizing and retrieving human knowledge. Bush described *memex* as a privatized, mechanized archive – a kind of desk where an individual could store all their books, records and communications. Its true novelty, however, does not lie in the mere possibility of storage, but in the principle of associative indexing. This mechanism, opposed to rigid alphabetical or numerical classification systems, was supposed to imitate the non-linear and jumpy connections created by the human mind. The user could create traces or paths between different documents, thus linking related ideas and data regardless of their original form or location. It was this idea of ​​interconnected information that directly inspired the later creators of hypertext, such as Ted Nelson and Douglas Engelbart, and today is reflected in the very structure of the World Wide Web.
Almost at the same time, Norbert Wiener (1948) in his work *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine* lays the foundations of cybernetics, a scientific discipline that studies the principles of control and communication in living beings and machines. Wiener's thought is focused on the universal laws that govern complex systems, regardless of whether they are biological or mechanical in nature. At the center of cybernetic thought is the notion of a feedback loop (*feedback loop*), a fundamental mechanism of self-regulation that enables the system to adapt and maintain stability based on received information about its own actions. By studying how systems collect, process and use information to guide their behavior, cybernetics has provided a theoretical framework for the development of artificial intelligence, robotics and computer networks. She introduced the notion of a system as an agent that actively operates within its environment, thus laying the foundation stone for understanding machines that can learn and make decisions independently.
Although they operated in the period before the full digital revolution, Bush's vision of networked knowledge and Wiener's theory of self-managing information systems form the two fundamental pillars on which the architecture of the information society was later built. Bush predicted *how* we would access information, while Wiener explained *how* systems, including technological ones, could manage that information. Their work testifies to the fact that the information society is primarily the result of intellectual evolution, and only then of the technological revolution.

## 2.7 Internet and digital revolution
At the very turn of the 20th and 21st centuries, at a time when it seemed that the fundamental communication challenges had already been solved by the establishment of fast and global connections via telephone, radio and television, a completely new communication ecosystem appeared on the horizon: the Internet. In a period of just a few decades, this system, originally conceived as an experimental project of the American agency ARPA (*Advanced Research Projects Agency*), has grown into a fundamental infrastructure for information exchange, collaboration and social change (Leiner et al., 2009).
Its origin, ARPANET, was designed as a research network to connect scientists and universities across the United States. Among the first institutions to join the project were the University of California (UCLA) and the Stanford Research Institute (SRI), which enabled their researchers to exchange data and coordinate experimental work much faster. In 1969, the first, now legendary network message was sent. The attempt to send the word "LOGIN" was interrupted after entering the letter "LO" due to a system crash, but this technical failure was recorded as a symbolic beginning of a new era. What in the early seventies seemed like a technological idea reserved for a narrow circle of the scientific community, soon transformed into a fundamental invention that will mark the entire subsequent period of digital communication.

![](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch02_arpanet.svg)
*Figure 2.21: Schematic representation of the development of the ARPANET: from the ARPA research network and university connectivity to the first network message in 1969 and the foundations of digital communication.*

A turning point in its development took place in 1989, when Tim Berners-Lee, a scientist employed at CERN, designed the *World Wide Web* (Berners-Lee, 1990) and thus transformed it into a platform accessible to every user. Using hypertext links and simple Internet browsers, people were given the opportunity for the first time to navigate distant digital spaces, simultaneously sharing documents, images, and even early forms of multimedia content.
The power of the Internet is not only manifested in its technical basis, but primarily in its ability to fundamentally reshape social, economic and cultural paradigms. By enabling an almost instantaneous and unhindered exchange of information on a global scale, the Internet has abolished previous spatial and temporal limitations, establishing a new information society in which access to knowledge and data becomes a key resource (Castells, 2010). This has dramatically changed the ways in which individuals and communities communicate, work and spend their free time, creating entirely new forms of social interaction and cultural expression.
The development of online platforms and tools in the last three decades has proven to be the central driver of profound transformations in the way people communicate with each other, collaborate and jointly shape social relationships. Although these processes have opened up enormous opportunities for networking, exchange of ideas and the creation of global communities, at the same time they have fueled numerous controversies, primarily those related to issues of privacy and the quality of newly formed relationships.
In economic terms, the digital revolution fueled by the Internet has led to the creation of new markets and business models that were unimaginable in the analog age. E-commerce, the sharing economy and remote working are just a few examples of disruptive innovations that have transformed entire industries. In such an environment, the role and skills of business agents also had to adapt. Successful operation in the modern market today implies a high level of digital literacy - the ability to navigate the digital ecosystem, use specialized tools and understand new forms of market communication.
Although the reach of the digital revolution is undoubtedly enormous, it would be wrong to ignore its darker sides and the challenges it poses to modern society. One of the fundamental problems is the so-called *digital divide*, i.e. the growing difference between those who have access to information technology and the benefits it provides and those who are excluded from this process due to economic, social or geographical reasons (Norris, 2001). Furthermore, ubiquitous digitization has also opened up complex issues of privacy protection, as vast sets of personal data are constantly being collected and processed.
In parallel, the ease of information dissemination via the Internet has created fertile ground for misinformation and malicious propaganda, which threatens the foundations of public debate and trust in institutions. The fight against fake news and cyber threats requires not only the development of new technological and regulatory frameworks but also the systematic strengthening of the media and digital literacy of the entire population. The ability to critically evaluate information therefore becomes a key skill for navigating the complexity of the digital age.
### 2.7.1 Expansion of communication channels
E-mail stands out among the first mass-accepted Internet services, which in the nineties of the 20th century introduced a radical shift in everyday business and personal information exchange. Unlike traditional mail and fax, e-mail has enabled instant delivery of messages regardless of geographic distance (Shapiro & Varian, 1999). For example, global companies have begun to connect their offices on different continents almost in real time, speeding up decision-making and raising the level of coordination between teams. At the same time, private users used e-mail to communicate with family and friends on a daily basis, making the world a smaller place at minimal cost and with unparalleled speed of data exchange.

Modern business completely changes the way companies communicate with clients: communication is moving away from a one-time sales transaction towards a complex process of building long-term relationships. Therefore, presence and availability on multiple communication platforms are no longer a competitive advantage, but a necessity. This approach is called multi-channel communication in professional literature and is considered one of the key factors for high client satisfaction. It includes traditional channels such as telephone and e-mail, but also extends to more dynamic platforms such as social networks, online *chat* services and mobile applications intended for users.

> **Multichannel communication:** Strategy of the simultaneous presence of an organization or agent on multiple communication platforms (telephone, e-mail, social networks, chat, mobile applications) in order to enable users to freely choose the channel that suits them best; a higher degree of integration, known as an omnichannel approach, aims for a unique and seamless user experience where the boundaries between channels are almost imperceptible.

Introducing a multi-channel approach brings significant business breakthroughs. It expands the reach and visibility of the company towards a wider circle of potential users and significantly enriches the overall user experience. Namely, clients are given the freedom to choose the communication path that suits them best at a given moment, which contributes to the feeling of comfort and adaptability of the service to their needs.
However, it should be emphasized that the implementation of a multi-channel strategy is a significant organizational issue. It requires the systematic education of agents who must be trained in the skillful use of all available tools. In doing so, it is crucial to ensure that messages and information provided to clients are aligned and consistent across all channels. Consistency in information and service quality, regardless of the chosen channel, becomes the basis of trust. Any deviation, i.e. providing contradictory or inconsistent answers, inevitably creates a feeling of insecurity and frustration in the client, which in the long run can damage the company's reputation.
Therefore, real and meaningful integration across multiple channels is actually set as the ultimate goal. This higher level of development, known as the **omnichannel** approach, implies the creation of a unique and flawless user experience in which the boundaries between individual channels are almost imperceptible. In a fully integrated system, the agent communicating with the customer has a complete view of all previous interactions, regardless of whether they took place via live chat, email or phone call. A client can start an inquiry on one platform, continue it on another and conclude on a third without having to repeat information, making the entire process fluid and user-centric.

### 2.7.2 Collaboration and collaborative tools
In the upgrade to e-mail, various collaborative systems such as *chat*-rooms (eng. *Chat*-*room*), forums and instant messages (eg ICQ, AIM, MSN *Messenger*) appeared. These early forms of digital exchange soon contributed to the development of innovative platforms for group work in the cloud, such as *Basecamp* or *Slack*, which significantly simplified team collaboration on projects, especially when it comes to software development, organization of international conferences or coordination of distance learning (Benkler, 2006).
The term cooperation in the context of multi-agent systems means the basic principle of action in which two or more agents, be they artificial or human, coordinate their activities in order to achieve a common goal. Cooperation is fundamentally different from coordination because it does not only mean avoiding conflict in action, but also active and purposeful exchange of information and resources. The basic premise of any cooperation is the existence of a common goal to which all involved agents strive. Achieving such a goal, which goes beyond the possibilities of individual action, necessarily implies a dynamic exchange of knowledge and information. This exchange enables agents to make better decisions.

> **Cooperation in multi-agent systems:** The principle of joint action of two or more agents (human or artificial) that coordinate activities to achieve a goal that exceeds the possibilities of individual action; it differs from mere coordination because it involves the active exchange of information and resources, negotiation and conflict resolution mechanisms, and the building of trust among participants (Benkler, 2006; Wooldridge, 2009).

However, the collaborative process is not without its challenges, among which the possibility of conflicting interests or agendas among the agents stands out. Therefore, the existence of pre-defined or dynamically established mechanisms for negotiation and conflict resolution is necessary for successful cooperation. These mechanisms ensure that partial interests are subordinated to the general goal and that the coherence of joint action is maintained.

In practical application, especially in complex environments where both humans and artificial agents operate, these principles are realized by using **collaborative** tools. These are software solutions and platforms designed to facilitate and structure interaction between participants. Their function is manifested on several levels: from tools for simultaneous editing and sharing of documents, through systems for delegating and monitoring tasks, all the way to communication platforms that enable asynchronous and synchronous communication. The role of these tools is to encourage and maintain trust, which is crucial for any long-term and fruitful cooperation.

> **Collaborative tools:** Software solutions and platforms designed for the structured joint action of multiple participants - from tools for simultaneous editing of documents and file sharing, through systems for delegation and monitoring of tasks, to communication platforms for synchronous and asynchronous exchange; in the context of multi-agent systems, collaborative tools encourage and maintain trust and enable effective coordination between human and artificial agents (Benkler, 2006).
### 2.7.3 Mass communication and homogenization of culture
Mass communication, as one of the fundamental processes that shape modern societies, represents a complex phenomenon whose influence extends to all aspects of social life, and especially to the field of culture. By means of technological innovations - from print and radio to television and the Internet - channels were created that enabled almost simultaneous transmission of messages to a vast, spatially dispersed and heterogeneous crowd. In this process, the mass media are not only channels for the transmission of information, but act as powerful *agents* of social change, actively participating in the creation, shaping and dissemination of cultural patterns, values ​​and worldviews.
The central consequence of such action, which is hotly debated in sociological and cultural thought, is the **homogenization** of culture. It is a process of gradual reduction of cultural diversity and suppression of autochthonous, local cultural expressions under the influence of dominant, globally expanded cultural contents. Mass communication, in its essence, becomes the main bearer and mediator of these dominant contents, thereby creating a global shared symbolic environment. Unique cultural patterns, which originate predominantly from Western, especially Anglo-American cultural centers, spread on a planetary level and are adopted in different parts of the world.
This process is most clearly manifested in several key domains. In the field of audiovisual culture, the Hollywood film industry and the global music scene impose aesthetic standards, narrative structures and musical genres that become universally recognizable and accepted. Similarly, in the sphere of consumer culture, global brands and marketing campaigns promote unique lifestyles, fashion trends, and consumer habits, erasing regional and national particularities in dress, diet, and everyday practices (Thompson, 1995).
The influence on **language**, as the fundamental bearer of cultural identity, is particularly significant. The spread of English as a global *lingua franca* through media content leads to the mass adoption of English in national languages. This seemingly unequivocal linguistic borrowing becomes a process that can affect the deeper semantic and syntactic structures of the language, changing the ways in which thoughts and feelings are expressed within a particular language community. This exposes linguistic diversity, one of humanity's greatest assets, to the pressure of unification.



However, it would be wrong to consider the process of cultural homogenization as one-way and absolute. Contemporary theories of globalization warn of the existence of resistance and counter-tendencies, whereby local cultures often actively reinterpret global influences, adapt them to their own contexts, and combine them with indigenous elements. This complex process is called hybridization or glocalization - a phenomenon in which the global and the local intertwine, creating new, unique cultural forms (Robertson, 1994). For example, globally popular music genres are enriched with traditional instruments or local chants, and international television formats are adapted to national humor and social specifics.

*Figure 2.24: Presentation of the relationship between mass communication, homogenization and glocalization of culture - media and ideational landscapes and the hybridization of the global and the local.*


The relationship between mass communication and culture remains inherently dialectical. While the mass media undoubtedly possess enormous potential for unifying cultural practices and values ​​on a global scale, they also provide platforms for the affirmation and visibility of local identities. Cultural homogenization therefore remains a permanent tension between the forces of global integration and the need to preserve local distinctiveness, a tension that will decisively shape the cultural landscape of the 21st century.

### 2.7.4 Political sphere and electronic public opinion
The emergence and pervasiveness of the Internet fundamentally transformed the landscape of political communication, introducing new paradigms in shaping, expressing and measuring public opinion. The digital age has put an end to the former exclusive dependence of political actors on traditional mass media as intermediaries in the transmission of messages to voters. Instead of a one-way channel, political communication now takes place within a complex, multi-directional and interactive network structure. In this new environment, a phenomenon that we call *electronic public opinion* - a dynamic and often elusive sum of attitudes, feelings and opinions expressed on digital platforms such as social networks, forums, blogs and portals with comments - arises and operates.
The key determinant of this transformation is the change in the role of the citizens themselves. From the position of mostly passive recipients of political messages, they become active *agents* in the political process. The ability to instantly comment, share content, create their own narratives and organize collective actions gives them power that they did not have in the pre-internet era. This displaces the political discussion from formalized frameworks, such as parliament or media debates, into a much wider, more open, but also more chaotic space of the Internet. In such a space, official political narratives confront unfiltered experiences, criticisms and alternative viewpoints of citizens, which creates a polyphonic, but also polarized public sphere. As Castells (2009) notes, power in the information age is increasingly found in communication networks, and the ability to influence public discourse becomes a key resource of political struggle.

Furthermore, electronic public opinion has properties that significantly differentiate it from that measured by traditional survey research. Its main feature is the speed of creation and changeability; it reacts to events in real time and can change drastically within hours under the influence of new information or viral content. It is also more transparent in its creation because it is based on publicly available data - posts, comments, likes and shares. However, these very properties make it susceptible to manipulation. Organized groups, be it political parties, interest lobbies or foreign actors, can use automated accounts (*bots*) or coordinated campaigns to create a false impression of mass support or resistance to an idea, law or candidate.
The structure of the Internet public sphere also brings other challenges. In contrast to Habermas' ideal image of a unified public sphere in which rational discussion leads to consensus, the digital space is extremely fragmented. Users tend to gather in like-minded communities, creating so-called *echo chambers* or *filter bubbles*, where they are exposed only to those information and attitudes that confirm their existing beliefs (Sunstein, 2017). Such informational isolation makes it difficult for different social groups to understand each other and strongly contributes to political polarization. The speed and reach of digital communication, although carrying enormous potential for democratization, at the same time open up space for the unprecedented spread of misinformation and fake news, the verification of which for the average user represents a significant cognitive effort. The political sphere consequently faces a double challenge: on the one hand, it must adapt to new forms of citizen participation and use their democratic potential, while on the other hand, it must develop mechanisms to defend against manipulations and preserve the quality of public debate in the digital environment.

### 2.7.5 Global spread and cultural influence
The universal networking of the modern world, achieved through digital technologies and the Internet, is a fundamental prerequisite for the global dissemination of cultural content and ideas. This digital infrastructure breaks down spatial and temporal barriers, enabling the almost instantaneous dissemination of information at an unprecedented level. This creates frameworks within which cultural phenomena, regardless of their origin, acquire the potential for planetary distribution.
Various agents operate within this newly established communication framework, such as social media platforms, influential individuals (*influencers*) and specialized internet communities. Their role on the superficial level is manifested in mediating or distributing content, but they are essentially active participants in the processes of creating, adapting and reshaping the meaning of cultural products. Through their action, the contents are spread, but also interpreted, commented on and incorporated into new semantic networks.
As already mentioned, globally available cultural phenomena go through the process of localization, i.e. adaptation to specific cultural, linguistic and social contexts. This combination of the global and the local is called *glocalization*, and it manifests itself, for example, in the way internet memes are translated and adapted to resonate with local humor and social circumstances (Shifman, 2014). The content thus retains its recognizable core, but at the same time takes on new, locally determined meanings.
Such permeation is the origin of hybrid cultural forms that combine elements of different traditions. This eliminates the sharp dichotomy between, for example, Western and non-Western culture, because cultural influence becomes multidirectional and mutual. The global scene is increasingly becoming an arena where different cultural expressions meet, mix and create new, syncretic forms that testify to the complexity of contemporary identities.
The described processes also challenge simplistic views such as technological determinism - the point of view according to which technology unequivocally shapes society and culture. On the contrary, it is evident that users and communities actively appropriate technology, adapt it to their needs and use it in unforeseen ways, thereby becoming agents of cultural and social change themselves (Baym, 2015). ### 2.7.6 Expansion of social networks
After the consolidation of the fast digital communication paradigm, the late 1990s and early 2000s saw the emergence of the early generation of social networks, such as *SixDegrees.com* (launched in 1997). Named after the concept of *six degrees of separation*, suggesting that any two people on Earth can be connected by a chain of six or fewer acquaintances, the site allowed users to create profiles, connect to lists of friends and family, effectively laying the groundwork for the concept of online social networking. Although initially limited in reach, *SixDegrees* paved the way for later mega-platforms such as *MySpace* (2003) and *Facebook* (2004), on which the momentum of mass online socializing was traced (Boyd & Ellison, 2007). The real turning point was the appearance of *Facebook* in 2004, a platform that fundamentally changed the paradigm of digital interaction. The success of that network rested on a previously new approach: instead of creating anonymous virtual identities, the focus was placed on mapping and establishing existing social connections within the digital environment. This encouraged the use of real names and connecting with people from the user's immediate social reality, which created a sense of authenticity and trust that previous networks lacked (Boyd & Ellison, 2007).

Further diversification of forms of digital communication was manifested in the emergence of *Twitter*, which introduced a concise microblogging format. By limiting messages to a small number of characters, it enabled an unprecedented speed of flow of information, news and personal announcements in real time. At the same time, technological progress, primarily the widespread availability of smartphones, has directed development towards mobile applications and visual content. Platforms like *Instagram* and *Snapchat* built their foundations precisely on the exchange of photos and short videos. They thus went beyond the mere function of a communication tool and became prominent spaces for the construction and expression of personal and collective identity (Van Dijck, 2013).
Development also went in the direction of functional specialization, which is most evident in the establishment of professional networks such as *LinkedIn*, which have become an indispensable arena for career development, business networking and presentation of professional achievements. The contemporary moment is marked by the predominance of platforms based on short video format, among which *TikTok* stands out. Its peculiarity lies not only in its content, but primarily in the way it is distributed, where the algorithmic system takes a central role. In such a model, the algorithm becomes the key agent that mediates between the content and the user, actively and constantly shaping his entire experience with personalized recommendations.
Finally, it is important to point out that the implications of the expansion of social networks are social, cultural and political. These platforms have assumed the role of central arenas for public discourse, political organizing and social activism, and at the same time have become fertile ground for the systematic spread of misinformation and manipulative content (Zuboff, 2019). Therefore, their influence on contemporary society should be understood in all its complexity and contradictions, as a phenomenon that reshapes the ways in which we communicate, work, create communities and understand ourselves.

### 2.7.7 Digital Tribes and Knowledge Squares: Anatomy of Successful Online Communities
Soon after the initial formation of broad and general digital markets, the Internet landscape began to blossom into specialized ecosystems—virtual communities dedicated to creating, sharing, evaluating, and discussing very specific types of content or interests. Platforms have become fascinating social laboratories where users gather, developing their own governance mechanisms, reputation systems, and unique microcultures that shape behavior and interaction within their digital walls.
Take *Reddit* for example. That platform is not so much a single community as a vast, *sprawling* digital metropolis composed of thousands, if not millions, of thematic sub-communities known as *subreddits*. Each *subreddit* functions as a separate neighborhood or even a miniature digital city-state, often with its own carefully crafted set of rules, norms of behavior, and a team of volunteer moderators who act as a kind of local government (Massanari, 2017). The spectrum is incredible: from strictly moderated academic forums like *r/science* or *r/AskHistorians*, where detailed references and in-depth analysis are required, to chaotic and humorous corners like *r/funny* or niche communities dedicated to the narrowest possible interests. What gives *Reddit* its dynamism is its basic *upvote/downvote* mechanism, which acts as a collective curation machine: content that the community finds valuable or interesting rises to the top, while less relevant or low-quality content is relegated to the digital background. It is a continuous referendum on relevance.
If we compare *Reddit* to a bustling city with countless different neighborhoods, then *Stack Overflow* (part of the wider *Stack Exchange* network) is its precisely organized technical library, or perhaps a highly specialized guild home for developers. Born from the need for quick and accurate answers to specific technical questions, *Stack Overflow* has become an almost unavoidable reference point in the world of software development (Mamykina et al., 2011). Its success lies in its rigorous focus on quality and meritocracy. The system of scoring (reputation) and accepting the best answers creates a strong incentive to provide accurate, clear and useful solutions. The community actively maintains a high signal-to-noise ratio, discouraging vague questions or opinion-based answers, creating an extremely efficient mechanism for collective problem-solving and archiving of specific technical knowledge.
*Discord* followed a different evolutionary path. Originally launched as a tool for voice communication among video game players, it quickly outgrew its initial niche and became a platform for a wide range of communities (Seering et al., 2019). Unlike asynchronous forums like *Reddit* or *Stack Overflow*, *Discord* offers a sense of real-time presence and interaction, more akin to a network of vibrant digital clubs or community hubs. Its server-based structure, with channels organized by topic (text, voice, video) and the ability to integrate bots for task automation and moderation, has proven to be extremely flexible. Today, it is used not only by players, but also by educational institutions, art collectives, support groups, and even companies for internal communication and external community building, creating spaces for more fluid and immediate interaction.
Finally, we must not forget open-source projects and collaborative repositories like *GitHub*. Although they are not social networks in the classical sense, platforms like *GitHub* function as huge digital construction sites and workshops, and at the same time as powerful communication networks that connect millions of developers and technical experts around the world (Dabbish et al., 2012). The combination of code versioning tools (such as Git), issue trackers, pull requests, and integrated discussion forums creates a highly structured communication framework optimized for complex technical collaboration. The success of projects like the operating system *Linux*, the *web*-framework *React* or the AI ​​library *TensorFlow* testifies to the incredible power of this model of distributed collective intelligence, where thousands of individuals, often without direct contact, manage to build together some of the most complex software artifacts today.
*GitHub* in this new ecosystem of machine-machine collaboration turns into a kind of operational base of autonomous code agents: Google's Jules, for example, opens *pull-requests* and automatically suggests corrections based on the analysis of the complete repository, relying on *GitHub*'s API for versioning and code review flows. A similar symbiosis is already demonstrated by *OpenAI-*ev *Codex *– the engine that powers *Copilot* – because it learns from public repositories and generates ready-made code in a format that can be immediately incorporated into those same projects. New tools called *vibe-coding* (eng. *vibe-coding*) like *Windsurf* or *Cursor* go a step further: the agent analyzes the history of *commits*, writes tests and runs CI *pipelines*, making *GitHub* a shared working memory through which people and models are coordinated in real time. Such an infrastructure allows software development to gradually move from manual code editing to process orchestration, where people set goals and a distributed *agent swarm* on *GitHub* realizes tasks – from bug discovery to documentation writing – with transparent audit history and collective approval mechanisms.
These diverse examples – from the city-states of *Reddit* and the guilds of *Stack Overflow*, through the clubs of *Discord* to the global construction sites of *GitHub* – illustrate the richness and inventiveness of digital forms of association in *online* communities. In these virtual spaces, individuals, driven by common interests, passions or goals, establish lasting connections, build social capital and participate in the collective creation of value (Rheingold, 2000). In order to penetrate into the anatomy of their success, it is necessary to look at the fundamental elements that make them stable and purposeful.
A fundamental prerequisite for the creation and survival of any living *online* community is the existence of a strong and clearly defined *common interest*. Whether it's professional collaboration, hobbyist passion or mutual support, it's this central gathering point that gives the community its identity and meaning. It acts as a gravitational center that attracts new members and retains existing ones, ensuring internal cohesion. Without this common purpose, the community dissipates into a multitude of unrelated interactions and loses its reason for existence.
The key pillar of every successful community is built on this foundation: *mutual trust and social capital*. Trust is not a given, it is gradually acquired through repeated positive interactions, reliability and respect for established norms. The accumulation of such experiences creates social capital, that is, a network of relationships and reciprocity that enables members to cooperate effectively and feel safe (Putnam, 2000). In such an environment, members are more willing to share valuable information, openly ask for help, and invest their own time and effort for the benefit of the whole.
Each digital tribe develops its own system of *rules and norms of behavior*, whether they are explicitly codified in the form of forum rules or exist as tacit, implicit agreements. These rules regulate communication, resolve conflicts and protect the community from destructive behavior. In addition to formal rules, the development of specific *language, jargon and symbols* that are understandable only to members is of crucial importance. Such an internal code not only facilitates and accelerates communication on complex topics, but also strongly strengthens the sense of belonging and collective identity, clearly demarcating "us" from "them".

The role of *agents*, both human and software, in maintaining community health is extremely important. Human agents, such as *moderators* or *community managers*, act as gardeners of the digital space: they encourage discussion, guide new members, calm tensions and remove unwanted content. Their presence and active engagement guarantee that the community remains a safe and productive space. On the other hand, software agents, through algorithms for filtering, recommending content or connecting users with similar interests, automate key functions and allow the community to scale to a large number of members.
Finally, the success of a community is reflected in the value it creates for its members and, sometimes, for the general public. This value can take different forms: from solving specific problems and *sharing professional knowledge* to providing *emotional support* and a sense of belonging. In their most developed forms, *online* communities become the scene of the emergence of *collective intelligence*, where the synergy of individual contributions leads to insights and solutions that exceed the capabilities of any individual (Lévy, 1997). Seen in this way, *online* communities stop being just a technological phenomenon and become key factors in the knowledge society, capable of solving complex challenges through collaboration and trust built in the digital world.

### 2.7.8 Porous membranes: when digital becomes real (and vice versa)
Once upon a time, the Internet was thought of as a separate space, a cybernetic world that one entered and exited from. But as digital platforms became more deeply woven into the fabric of our everyday lives, that boundary began to melt, becoming increasingly porous, like a membrane through which ideas, relationships, and identities constantly penetrate in both directions. Virtual communities were no longer just remote sanctuaries; their inhabitants began to spill over into the physical world, organizing meetups, conferences, protests, and get-togethers that blurred the distinction between *online* acquaintances and *offline* friends (Rainie & Wellman, 2012). The arrival of smartphones, those ubiquitous portals in our pockets that ensure our constant connection, has only ended the last illusions about a clear dividing line. Our *online* and *offline* lives are no longer parallel worlds; they are inextricably intertwined.
Platforms like *Meetup.com* have become a perfect example of this phenomenon. They function as digital catalysts for physical interaction, bringing together people with similar interests – whether they're rare dog lovers, programmers learning a new language, activists organizing a cleanup, or book club members. The initial contact and group formation happens *online*, but the real goal is to meet face-to-face: gathering in coffee shops, parks, libraries or workshops (Quan-Haase & Wellman, 2002). There, abstract avatars and text conversations turn into real handshakes, shared laughs and lively discussions, demonstrating how the digital can enrich, not just replace, physical sociality and community building. It is a picture of networked individualism where connections are formed around common interests, regardless of geographic proximity, but often materialize in physical space (Wellman, 2001).
However, this blurring of boundaries has not gone without causing deep discomfort and serious questions. While some celebrated new forms of connection, others expressed concerns about the authenticity of relationships nurtured through screens and the potential for the erosion of privacy in a world of constant digital surveillance. Is the person we know through a carefully curated *online* profile the same person we meet in person? In her influential works, Sherry Turkle warned of the phenomenon of alone together, where technology creates the illusion of connection while actually isolating us, encouraging us to prefer orderly, controlled online interactions over the messy unpredictability of real human relationships (Turkle, 2011).
Privacy concerns have exploded into the public consciousness with scandals such as *Cambridge Analytica*, which exposed how personal data collected from *Facebook* (often without user consent or understanding) can be used for sophisticated psychological profiling and targeted political persuasion, potentially influencing the outcome of elections (Cadwalladr & Graham-Harrison, 2018). This and similar incidents have illuminated the background of the business models of many platforms, based on what Shoshana Zuboff calls surveillance capitalism: the extraction and monetization of human experience transformed into data, often through unclear terms of use and interfaces designed to encourage us to share more than we would consciously consent to (Zuboff, 2019). Our *online* activity, our likes, clicks, and even keeping our eyes on certain content, become raw material for algorithms that profile us and predict our behavior, shaping the information we see and the products we are offered.
At the same time, the constant presence of the digital world has fueled discussions about digital alienation, addiction and the impact on mental health. The design of many social networks, with endless scrolling, notifications, and variable reward mechanisms (like likes), can create compulsive behavior loops (Alter, 2017). The pressure to maintain a perfect virtual identity, constant comparison with others and fear of missing out (FOMO - *Fear Of Missing Out*) are associated with increased anxiety and depression, especially among younger users (Twenge, 2019). The line between using technology as a tool and becoming its prisoner is becoming dangerously thin.
Thus, while the Internet has undoubtedly broken down barriers and enabled new forms of community and collaboration, it has simultaneously created a complex and often ambivalent space in which our digital and physical lives are constantly interwoven.
The dividing line between the digital and the real world, once thought of as a solid and insurmountable border, today is increasingly manifested as a porous membrane. Through it, a constant and two-way permeation takes place, which changes the ontological status of both worlds. It is no longer about the interaction of two separate entities, but about the creation of a hybrid space in which digital bits materialize and physical reality is translated into information code. This process of mutual overflow questions fundamental assumptions about the nature of reality, presence and action.

### 2.7.9 Digital revolution and global communication network
The entry into the digital age has brought an atmosphere of continuous connectivity and accelerated global interactions, whereby the Internet and digital tools have become the logistical and cultural fabric of modern society. The shift from physical infrastructure and traditional forms of business to virtual ecosystems has created an environment where collaboration takes place independent of time and space, and the range of information is available on a scale that would have seemed unimaginable just a few decades ago. Such pervasiveness of communication and information flows continues civilization's long line of innovation, but now it seems that the pace of change has intensified to the extent that it is fundamentally transforming many aspects of human experience.
In the context of business and work processes, the construction of virtual market platforms illustrates these deeper changes. Examples of companies like *Amazon* and *eBay* show how the global distribution of goods and services has entered a new phase, characterized by hybrid models that connect traditional and digital trade modalities. At the same time, the practice of collaboration through online applications for conversations, document sharing and video conferencing has spread. By selecting collaborators regardless of their geographical location, organizations are increasingly fostering "dispersed teams", in which knowledge merges into a single whole. These practices parallel the accelerated "cultural globalization", in which local work culture meets universal standards of digital collaboration, bringing new challenges to organizations that strive to harmonize various work traditions with the fluidity of virtual collaboration.

Education has undergone a similar turn. *Online* courses and open educational resources provide access to content previously available mostly at elite institutions. The great popularity of platforms like *Courser*, *edX* and *Khan Academy* shows that the demand for flexible, adaptable and above all affordable forms of learning is constantly increasing. Many students and professionals for whom formal, long-term studies are not an option are now choosing microcolleges and specialized courses to adapt more quickly to the changing demands of the labor market or to follow their own interests in the arts, sciences or humanities.
Changes in the media sphere are equally diverse. Increasing digital competition has put traditional newspapers, television companies and radio stations in front of the task of radical adaptation to new communication customs and advertising models. With the appearance of internet channels, blogs, *podcasts* and platforms for *videostreaming*, space has opened up for countless forms of creative expression. Discussions now take place on Internet forums (Rheingold, 1993), where professional knowledge or niche passions are exchanged, and news broadcasts are often replaced by interactive broadcasts in real time, whereby the audience experienced a transformation from a passive observer to an active participant.

New economic circumstances were by no means absent. The concept of the sharing economy and platforms like *Uber* and *Airbnb* (Sundararajan, 2016) have shaken some established rules about ownership, rental and work, showing how agile digital mediation is in connecting supply and demand, even if it raises issues of regulation and protection of workers' rights. At the same time, open collaboration movements have reached a peak in *open-source* projects, where global communities develop complex software solutions without traditional hierarchical structures (Benkler, 2006). Such practice has shown that knowledge and innovation can flourish when monopolistic controls are broken and resources are freely available.

Similar collective mechanisms appear in the sphere of financing. *Crowdfunding* campaigns through platforms like *Kickstarter* and *Indiegogo* (Howe, 2008) reveal how a wider audience, not exclusively banks or corporate investors, takes on the role of micro-investors in creative and entrepreneurial projects. Such initiatives revitalize a culture of personal engagement, where supporters donate funds and spread the word about new ideas and actively build a community around them. In one dimension, this brings the empowerment of small innovators, and in another, it imposes the need to restore trust and transparency: in a digital world full of information, it is easy and uncertain to assess who offers what value and with what motives.
All these phenomena point to the fact that the digital era, continuing the centuries-old traditions of communication evolution, is becoming a multi-layered experience – intimate and global, innovative and disruptive, promising and risky. It enables intense collaboration and great feats of knowledge discovery, but requires thoughtful strategies for managing privacy, ethics, and intellectual property. It opens up new frontiers for business, education and media, but invites in-depth discussions about the balance of power in the digital space and the control of infrastructure and resources. At the root of all these developments remains the key understanding that communication, for centuries now, is the guiding thread of the development of civilization, and digital technologies are only the last act in the continuous performance of human creativity and interaction.
### 2.7.10 Digital duality: the promise of democratization and the shadow of new inequalities
The entry into the digital age is marked by a fundamental tension, a duality that permeates almost every aspect of modern life. Digital technology is the scene of conflicting aspirations that simultaneously offer the promise of liberation and cast the shadow of new forms of subjugation. *Digital duality* manifests itself as a permanent conflict between the vision of a democratized world, in which knowledge and power are available to everyone, and the actual appearance of new, often invisible, inequalities that deepen existing ones and create new social cleavages. Understanding this duality is crucial for any serious judgment of our time.
On the one hand, the digital revolution has enormous democratization potential. By breaking down spatial and temporal barriers, the Internet and its related technologies have created unprecedented opportunities for access to information and knowledge. Global libraries, scientific databases and educational platforms have become available to individuals regardless of their geographical location or material status, promoting the idea of ​​a global republic of knowledge. In the political arena, digital media have enabled new forms of civic participation and activism. The ability to quickly organize, share information outside the control of traditional media and directly address power holders gave a voice to those who rarely had one. The potential is manifested in the ability of social movements to quickly mobilize and articulate their demands, creating a new form of what Manuel Castells (2012) calls “network power”. In economic terms, digital platforms have lowered the entry barriers for entrepreneurship, allowing small producers and creators access to a global market that was once reserved exclusively for large corporations.

On the other side of this bright picture, however, a dark shadow looms. The promise of full democratization remains largely unfulfilled, while at the same time new, more subtle mechanisms of surveillance and inequality are being established. The first and most obvious is the *digital divide*, which no longer refers only to mere access to the Internet, but also to differences in digital literacy, skills and the ability to critically evaluate information. Those without the necessary skills or resources remain excluded from the digital society, further deepening their existing marginalization.
Furthermore, the architecture of the digital world is increasingly shaped by algorithms that, rather than being impartial arbiters, often reflect and reinforce human biases. Algorithmic systems that decide on employment, credit approval or even the probability of committing a criminal offense can systematically discriminate against certain social groups, creating what Cathy O'Neil (2016) calls "weapons of math destruction". These automated systems act as invisible bureaucracies, whose decisions are difficult to challenge because they are shrouded in a veil of technical objectivity and business secrecy.
Perhaps the most profound threat is the rise of what Shoshana Zuboff (2019) calls *surveillance capitalism*. In this economic model, human experience becomes a raw material that is freely appropriated, turned into behavioral data, and sold in markets that trade predictions about our future. The power is thereby extremely unevenly distributed: on the one hand, there are technological giants who possess the knowledge and infrastructure for collecting and processing data, and on the other hand, users, individual agents who are often not even aware of the extent of surveillance to which they are exposed. This asymmetry of power undermines the very idea of ​​autonomy and free decision-making, creating a market in which we are not the product, but the possibility of influencing our future behavior.
Digital duality is therefore a real tension that shapes our social, political and economic reality, revealing the digital sphere not as a utopian or dystopian destination but as a battlefield. The outcome of this struggle depends on the actions of various agents: on the regulatory decisions of states, on the efforts of civil society in the protection of digital rights, on the ethical responsibility of technological creators and, finally, on raising awareness and critical engagement of the users themselves. Acknowledging this duality means rejecting technological determinism and accepting responsibility for actively shaping the digital future—a future in which the promise of democratization will overcome the shadow of new inequalities.

### 2.7.11 Shadows in a digital Eden: privacy, manipulation and the price of connectivity
The semblance of a digital Eden, a world of unlimited possibilities and universal connectivity, brings with it its shadows, spaces where basic human rights and cognitive autonomy are exposed to unprecedented pressures. At the root of this duality lies the paradigm of surveillance capitalism, an economic order that appropriates human experience as a free raw material for production and sales processes (Zuboff, 2019). The first and most obvious victim of such a system is privacy. Massive and ubiquitous data collection – from location and search patterns to biometric information and emotional reactions – has become a fundamental mechanism for the functioning of the digital space. Users, often without full awareness of the scale and depth of this process, willingly give up fragments of their lives in exchange for seemingly free services, thus becoming raw material in a value chain whose ultimate purpose is to monetize their future behavior.
Collected data traces are of course used to optimize services or place customized ads, but their purpose inevitably extends deep into the sphere of behavioral modification and targeted manipulation. Algorithmic systems, acting as invisible agents, construct personalized realities based on detailed psychographic profiles, worlds tailored to evoke certain emotional responses and stimulate desired actions. The scale and consequences of such actions became evident in the case of the company *Cambridge Analytica*, which tried to influence the outcome of democratic elections by misusing data collected from the social network *Facebook* (Cadwalladr & Graham-Harrison, 2018). This event exposed the systemic vulnerability of modern societies, showing how data infrastructure can be repurposed as a weapon for psychological and political shaping of the public.
![](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch02_nadzor_ciklus.svg)
*Figure 2.35: Systematic collection and analysis of user data leads to algorithmic profiling resulting in personalized content delivery and behavioral manipulation (surveillance capitalism cycle).*

The operation of these algorithms, whose fundamental task is the maximization of user engagement, leads to further structural disturbances in the flow of information. By creating so-called "filter bubbles" (eng. *filter bubbles*) and "echo chambers" (eng. *echo chambers*), which lock users inside information ecosystems, confirming their existing beliefs and worldviews (Pariser, 2011). By systematically suppressing opposing or divergent points of view, algorithmic curation of content erodes the space for critical reflection and public discussion, key prerequisites for a healthy democratic society. The result is social fragmentation and increased polarization, where the common ontological basis for dialogue is gradually disappearing.
The price of universal connection, apart from social and political, is also paid on a deeply personal, psychological level. Constant exposure to idealized depictions of other people's lives on social networks creates fertile ground for social comparison, which often results in feelings of inadequacy, anxiety and depression. The imperative of constant availability and participation in the digital sphere blurs the lines between work and leisure, public and private, creating pressures that negatively impact mental health and general well-being. The role of the digital agent – ​​whether it is a platform, an algorithm or an interface – is crucial in this ecosystem. Its goals, focused primarily on economic metrics such as retention and increased engagement, are often in direct conflict with the long-term interests and well-being of users. Therefore, facing the shadows of digital Eden requires the development of advanced digital literacy, strengthening awareness of the mechanisms that shape our online experience, and the establishment of solid regulatory frameworks that will protect the autonomy, privacy and dignity of the individual in the new digital age.

## 2.8 Beginnings of autonomous communication: the first digital interlocutors
The thought of creating artificial beings capable of conversation reaches deep into the human past, from mythical statues that come to life to the Golems of Prague legends. But it was only in the second half of the twentieth century, with the advent of computers, that this age-old aspiration began to take its first real, albeit rudimentary, forms. The transition from myth to technology was conditioned by a key theoretical shift offered by Alan Turing. In his famous work *Computing Machinery and Intelligence* (Turing, 1950), he did not ask whether machines can *think*, but whether they can *behave* in a way that is indistinguishable from humans. His thought experiment, known as the Turing test or the imitation game, established the philosophical and practical framework within which the field of artificial intelligence, and especially the field of natural language processing, would develop for decades. It was the challenge of creating a machine that could successfully conduct a written conversation with a human that became the driving force behind the development of the first digital interlocutors.
The first significant step on this path was taken by Joseph Weizenbaum, who created the ELIZA program in 1966. Acting on the principle of recognizing key words and applying simple transformation rules, ELIZA simulated a conversation. Her most famous script, called *DOCTOR*, imitated a Rogerian psychotherapist, using the technique of paraphrasing and asking open-ended questions taken from user input. For example, the program could respond to the statement "My family worries me" with "Tell me more about your family". Although technically simple, ELIZA caused an unexpectedly strong reaction. Users credited the program with understanding, empathy, and awareness, despite Weizenbaum's repeated claims that the machine understood nothing. This phenomenon, which he called the "ELIZA effect", revealed a deep human tendency to anthropomorphize and establish an emotional connection with even the simplest interlocutor, which deeply worried the author himself and prompted him to critically review the ethical limits of artificial intelligence (Weizenbaum, 1976).
![](../../docs/diagrams_en/diag_135.svg)
*Figure 2.37: Evolutionary steps of the first systems for language interaction: from ELIZE (1966) through PARRY (1972) and SHRDLU (1972) to the further development of dialogic agents and natural language processing.*

Shortly after ELIZA, psychiatrist Kenneth Colby from Stanford University developed the PARRY program in 1972. Unlike Weizenbaum's passive interlocutor, PARRY was designed as a more complex simulation, embodying the personality of a patient with paranoid schizophrenia. Colby's agent responded to stimuli, but also possessed an internal model of his own beliefs and affective states, such as fear, anger, and distrust, which influenced his responses. PARRY monitored the level of these affects and could proactively change the subject or become defensive if he felt the interlocutor was provoking him. In a series of tests, psychiatrists were often unable to distinguish transcripts of conversations with PARRY from those with human patients. As a historical curiosity, the conversation held in 1972 between ELIZA and PARRY over the ARPANET network, which clearly showed the limitations of both systems - the meeting of the passive mirror and the obsessive model resulted in a mostly incoherent and repetitive exchange.
At the same time, a different approach was developed, which was not aimed at simulating the human personality, but at the true understanding of the language within the given context. The most prominent example of such an approach was Terry Winograd's SHRDLU (1972). This system allowed the user to use natural language to issue commands to a virtual robotic arm in a "cube world". SHRDLU could understand complex and even ambiguous sentences, ask questions for clarification and remember the context of the conversation. For example, on the command "Pick up the big red cube" the system would execute it. If the user then said "Put it on green", SHRDLU would correctly understand that the pronoun "is" refers to the aforementioned red cube. Although limited to its microcosm, SHRDLU has shown that a deep understanding of semantics and syntax is possible, steering part of the research community towards knowledge-based models.
William Chamberlain and Thomas Etter developed a computer program designed to generate prose and poetry in English (Chamberlain & Etter, 1984). Racter, whose name comes from the word "raconteur" (storyteller), is said to have created the book *The Policeman's Beard Is Half Constructed* (published in 1984). According to the editorial framework of that publication, the text was presented as computer-generated, with minimal subsequent interventions, and the system used the rules of context-free grammar and elements of randomness to construct unique sentences and prose (Chamberlain & Etter, 1984; Adamopoulou & Mousiades, 2020).
The early systems, from ELIZA to PARRY and SHRDLU, seem technically naive from today's point of view, but their importance lies in the fact that they showed that machine processing of language was possible. They unraveled the psychological aspects of human-machine interaction and raised fundamental questions about the nature of intelligence, understanding and communication. These were the first, necessary steps in the dark, which, despite their obvious limitations, paved the way towards significantly more complex architectures and paradigms that will mark the next chapter – the world of large language models.
