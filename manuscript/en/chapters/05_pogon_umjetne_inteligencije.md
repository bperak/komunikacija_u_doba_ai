# 5. Artificial Intelligence Drive: Processing Power, the AI ​​Horizon, and the Birth of Digital Collectives
## 5.1 The Engine of Evolution: From Moore's Law to the AI ​​Revolution
At the origin of contemporary digital transformation lies a principle that has acted as an infallible metronome of technological progress for decades: **Moore's** law. Putting it forward in 1965, Gordon Moore, co-founder of Intel, predicted that the number of transistors on integrated circuits would double approximately every two years (Moore, 1965). This forecast, at first just an observation about a trend in the young semiconductor industry, turned into a self-fulfilling prophecy and became the fundamental economic and technological driver of the information age. The exponential growth of computing power, resulting from relentless miniaturization, was not just a technical achievement; it has created a predictable horizon for innovation, prompting the entire industry to continually invest in research and development to keep up with this set pace.
> **Moore's Law:** Empirical rule (Moore, 1965) according to which the number of transistors on an integrated circuit approximately doubles every two years; for decades it predicted the exponential growth of computing power and was the main driver of the IT industry.
However, in the second decade of the 21st century, it became apparent that the reign of Moore's Law was coming to an end. The laws of physics imposed limits on miniaturization at the atomic level, making further doubling of transistors increasingly difficult, expensive, and ultimately unsustainable. The slowing down of this, until then main, engine of progress could have meant stagnation, but instead it opened up space for the rise of a new paradigm and a new driver. That new engine became **artificial** intelligence.
The AI ​​revolution, particularly the rise of deep learning, has taken the legacy computing power enabled by Moore's Law and created an entirely new kind of demand, spawning the need for a different, specialized computing architecture. Classical processors (CPUs), optimized for the sequential execution of complex tasks, have proven insufficient for the massively parallel calculations required to train modern neural networks. The void was filled by **graphics** processing units (GPU). Originally intended for rendering computer graphics in video games, their ability to perform thousands of simple operations simultaneously proved perfect for the matrix calculations at the heart of deep learning.
> **GPU:** Graphics Processing Unit (eng. *Graphics Processing Unit*) – chip optimized for massively parallel calculations; it is crucial for training and running large neural networks and LLMs, because matrix operations require parallelism that the CPU offers to a lesser extent.
Thus, the demand for increasingly powerful AI models has become the main catalyst for innovation in computer hardware, taking over the role once played by Moore's Law. A new, powerful feedback loop has been created: advances in hardware enable the development of larger and more complex AI models, and the hunger of these models for computing power in turn encourages the creation of even more powerful and specialized hardware (Huang, 2017). That cycle isn't just limited to GPUs; tech giants like Google with their Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) and numerous other companies are now developing chips tailored exclusively for artificial intelligence.

This new dynamic is profoundly reshaping the technological landscape. While Moore's Law was characterized by fairly uniform advances in general-purpose power, the era of artificial intelligence is encouraging the diversity and specialization of hardware solutions. The engine of evolution is no longer single and centralized, but has dispersed into a complex interaction between software and hardware, between algorithmic discoveries and silicon innovations. This coupling directly affects the economy, scientific research and society as a whole, laying the foundations for a new technological revolution. Within this system, which is constantly and rapidly changing, **agents** work, be they human researchers, engineers or autonomous AI systems, constantly pushing the limits of what is possible.
## 5.2 Resource intensity: the price of intelligence
While they amaze us with their language abilities, the great language models come with an enormous, often glossed over price. Their impressive intelligence and performance are directly related to their monumental size and complexity, making them extremely resource-intensive systems. This intensity is manifested through three key aspects: the astronomical computational and financial costs required to train them, the worrying energy footprint and environmental impact, and the enormous memory requirements to run them. These factors present technical challenges for engineers, but also have significant economic, social and ethical implications. Namely, the questions are becoming louder: who can develop and use this powerful technology, and what is the long-term sustainability of the current trajectory of artificial intelligence development?

The first and most obvious cost is associated with training the most advanced language models. The pretraining process requires unimaginable amounts of computing power, possible only by using massive clusters of specialized hardware, primarily GPUs (like NVIDIA A100 or H100) or TPUs (Patterson et al., 2021). These clusters can contain thousands of accelerators running in parallel for weeks or months. Acquisition, maintenance and operation of such infrastructure results in direct financial costs that are measured in tens or even hundreds of millions of dollars for training just one *state-of-the-art* model. Such a high price creates a huge barrier to entering the market, leading to the concentration of power in the hands of a small number of the largest technology companies and creating a "compute divide" that can deepen existing inequalities.

Another, more and more recognized problem is the huge energy footprint and the associated impact on the environment. Intensive use of thousands of energy-demanding chips consumes enormous amounts of electricity. Studies have shown that training large models can emit significant amounts of carbon dioxide (Strubell et al., 2019; Luccioni et al., 2022). Although the estimates depend on many factors, including the source of electricity, they clearly indicate that the development of LLMs has a measurable contribution to climate change. Furthermore, the cumulative energy cost of inference should not be neglected either.
> **Inference:** Phase of using an already trained model – applying the model to new input data to obtain predictions or answers; for LLMs it means generating text based on a user query. It is energy and computationally demanding with millions of calls per day.
Although a single query is less energy demanding, the widespread use of LLMs, used by millions of users, means that the total energy consumption for inference can eventually exceed that spent on training (Luccioni et al., 2023). Increasing awareness of the issue is putting pressure on the industry to develop more sustainable practices, including optimizing efficiency and using renewable energy sources.
The third aspect of resource intensity is related to huge memory requirements. Storing the model parameters themselves (hundreds of billions or trillions) requires hundreds of gigabytes of memory. In addition, it is necessary to save temporary activations during the calculation. Especially in the inference of long sequences, the KV cache (*Key-Value Cache*), which stores vectors from the attention mechanism, can grow to very large dimensions, often exceeding the memory required for the parameters themselves (Pope et al., 2023). These memory requirements, especially for specialized and expensive memory on GPU/TPU accelerators (VRAM/HBM), represent a bottleneck. The consequence is that the largest models often cannot be run on a single accelerator, requiring complex parallelization techniques. More importantly, these requirements make it extremely difficult to run the most advanced LLMs directly on resource-constrained devices, such as smartphones or edge devices, limiting their availability and application. Optimization techniques such as quantization and cropping are key to alleviating this limitation.

Facing the aforementioned multiple challenges of resource intensity requires a multidisciplinary approach: further technical innovation in the development of more efficient algorithms and hardware, clear ethical guidelines and potentially regulatory frameworks that promote transparency and sustainability, and continuous dialogue between all stakeholders to ensure that the benefits of this powerful technology are available in a fair and sustainable manner.

## 5.3 Expanding horizons: from narrow tasks to digital collectives
At the very core of the development of artificial intelligence, driven by an unimaginable surge in computing power, a quiet but radical transformation is taking place. We are witnessing a journey that begins in the strictly limited boundaries of individual computer tasks, and strives towards the horizons of complex, dynamic worlds in which a multitude of digital beings operate and permeate. It's not just a series of new skills; it is the evolution of the very nature and organization of intelligence. In order to understand the depth of this change, it is necessary to follow its development, step by step, from solitary, narrowly focused agents to the birth of a new, almost organic entity of multiple artificially intelligent agents.

At the origin of that path stands a solitary agent to solve a narrow task; a master of his craft, but narrow-minded. Its power, no matter how great, is exhausted within the invisible walls of the algorithm that created it. A classic example of such a creature is DeepMind's AlphaGo, the grandmaster of the ancient game *go*. His genius, which surpassed the best minds of men, remains imprisoned on a wooden tablet with black and white stones; he cannot apply his knowledge to chess, nor can he recite a verse or interpret celestial changes. His intelligence is deep, but also hopelessly narrow, marked by a fate of insurmountable isolation.

The first real breakthrough from that isolation occurs with the appearance of an artificially intelligent agent that reaches for different tools. He overcomes his own limitations by learning to use external sources of knowledge and power, becoming a hub that skillfully collects and applies resources from the vast digital environment. This increases its effectiveness. Let's imagine a modern language model entrusted with the organization of travel. Since he himself does not know the real-time flight prices, he reaches for the available tools: he calls the airline's interface, uses an external calculator for currency conversion and checks the weather conditions at the destination through a dedicated service. Here, the agent did not solve every piece of the puzzle by himself, but like an excellent conductor orchestrated the concert of specialized tools, creating a harmonious whole.

But even such an agent remains a unique decision-making center. A true paradigm shift, the beginning of true digital sociality, occurs only with the establishment of multi-agent systems. In such an environment, multiple autonomous agents inhabit a common space, entering into mutual relations. The cornerstone that enables their existence is communication. It opens up the possibility for them to negotiate, coordinate their actions, share knowledge and jointly strive for a goal that exceeds the strength of each of them individually. Here, a complex task is broken down into parts, and a team of specialists works together to bring it to an end. Let's go back to vacation planning: instead of one conductor, we now have a team. Agent Researcher finds flights, Agent Analyst checks prices and reviews, Agent Coordinator reconciles passenger schedules, and Agent Evaluator keeps a close eye on possible risks. Their constant exchange of information, similar to the hum of a human work group, creates synergy and leads to a solution (Wang et al., 2023). Such cooperation, of course, requires enormous computing power, because every message, every coordination and every decision consumes precious resources of processing power.

The peak of this development, the point where quantity turns into a new quality, is manifested in the emergence of digital collectives. This is no longer the sum of individual parts, but a system that as a whole exhibits emergent properties - abilities and forms of behavior that cannot be found in any of its components. Like an anthill or a swarm of bees, the digital collective becomes more than a collection of its members; it develops collective memory, the ability of independent organization and learning at the level of the entire community.
> **Emergent properties:** Abilities, patterns or behaviors of a complex system that arise from the interaction of its components, and cannot be found in any of them separately; in the context of AI, they denote the emergence of new qualities (such as collective memory or self-organization) when multiple agents cooperate.
> **Digital collective:** A system of multiple autonomous AI agents that communicate and collaborate; as a whole it exhibits emergent properties – capabilities and behavior patterns that are not present in individual agents – analogous to social organizations in nature.
Research from Stanford University, which settled 25 generative agents in a virtual city, clearly showed how complex social patterns emerge from simple rules of interaction: the agents spread rumors on their own initiative, arranged meetings and created lasting relationships, without anyone explicitly instructing them to do so (Park et al., 2023). A small digital society was born.

And that germ of collective consciousness in a simulated society gets its full creative potential in projects like *Google DeepMind's AlphaEvolve*. This system embodies the archetype of the scientific collective of the future. In it, agents from the *Gemini* model family autonomously invent new algorithms. Some generate them, others test them in loops similar to Darwinian selection, and others carry out strict formal correctness checks. It is this approach, building on the successes of its predecessors such as *AlphaTensor*, that has enabled the discovery of new, proven more effective solutions to fundamental computing problems that people have been dealing with for decades. Here, the collective as a whole makes a creative leap, generating knowledge that is verifiably new and mathematically correct - it does not find, it creates.

Thus, before our eyes, the path from a solitary, lonely executor, through a skilled user of tools and a member of a collaborative team, all the way to a participant in an emergent, creative entity is drawn. Expanding the horizons of artificial intelligence is therefore not only a technological but also an ontological story. It leads us towards a new topology of knowledge, in which the boundaries between individual and community, tool and user, man and machine, begin to dissolve in the joint search for solutions. In these pulsating, self-learning collectives lies the promise of understanding worlds whose complexity has so far been beyond our reach.

## 5.4 Search for deeper understanding: emotional intelligence
As we build ever more capable AI systems, two long-term visions in particular capture the imagination and drive research, both inevitably linked to future advances in computing power and algorithms. One deals with the issue of emotional intelligence (EI) in AI. Can AI not only understand words, but also feel or at least convincingly simulate emotions? For example, can AI recognize traces of frustration or confusion in the user's voice (Picard, 1997) and adapt its way of communication, offer help or change its problem-solving strategy? EI is defined as a complex set of abilities that enables an individual to recognize, understand and manage their own emotional states, but also notice, interpret and influence the emotions of others. Although it has long been considered that cognitive intelligence, measured by the intelligence quotient (IQ), is a key predictor of success, today it is increasingly recognized that it alone is not sufficient to navigate the complexity of human relationships and life's challenges. Emotional intelligence is therefore not seen as the opposite but as a necessary complement to the analytical mind.
> **Emotional intelligence:** A complex set of abilities that enables an individual (or system) to recognize, understand and manage their own emotional states and notice, interpret and influence the emotions of others; according to Goleman (1995), it includes self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills.

The concept, although not entirely new, gained wide recognition through the work of Daniel Goleman (1995), who presented it as a key determinant of personal and professional fulfillment. Goleman's model, which became a kind of foundation for further research, breaks down emotional intelligence into five key components that interpenetrate and build on each other. According to him, the origin and foundation of everything is self-awareness – the ability to recognize and understand one's own feelings, moods and inner urges, as well as their impact on the environment. Self-awareness is directly connected to self-regulation, i.e. the skill of managing these internal states, controlling impulsive reactions, and acting in a deliberate manner instead of impulsively. The third component, motivation, is not seen in this framework as an external incentive but as an internal drive for achievement that goes beyond material rewards or social status; it is passion for work and perseverance in the face of obstacles. The fourth component, empathy, represents the transition from the inner world to the realm of interpersonal relations. It is the art of recognizing and empathizing with the emotional states of others, the ability to see the situation from another's perspective. Finally, social skills combine the previous components into an active ability to manage relationships, build networks of acquaintances, persuade and lead others.


![Golemanov model emocionalne inteligencije](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch05_goleman_ei.svg)
*Figure 5.3: Goleman's model of emotional intelligence - personal competencies (self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation) pass into social competencies (empathy, social skills).*
Ray Kurzweil boldly predicted that AI will achieve human-level emotional intelligence by 2029 (Kurzweil, 2005).
> **Affective computing:** An interdisciplinary field of research (eng. *affective computing*) that deals with the recognition, interpretation and simulation of emotions in computer systems; it includes analysis of facial expressions, voice and physiological signals and applications in empathic assistants, therapeutic and educational tools (Picard, 1997).
The field of affective computing has indeed advanced, with AI systems able to recognize basic emotions from facial expressions, voice or physiological signals with some accuracy (Li & Deng, 2020; El Ayadi, Kamel & Karray, 2011). The potential applications are vast – from empathic virtual assistants and therapists (Fitzpatrick, Darcy, & Vierhile, 2017) to educational tools that adapt to students' moods (D'Mello & Graesser, 2012).

However, achieving true emotional intelligence remains a huge challenge. This is not supported by the fact that human emotions are incredibly complex, contextual and subtle (Matsumoto & Hwang, 2012). Today's AI does not possess consciousness or subjective experience (Marcus & Davis, 2019), but only recognizes patterns. Is Kurzweil's deadline realistic? Many experts are skeptical (Boden, 2016; Searle, 1980), pointing out fundamental differences between simulation and real understanding and deep ethical issues related to the collection and potential manipulation of emotional data (Cowie, 2015; Floridi & Cowls, 2019). Despite skepticism, affective recognition serves as a potential reward signal for metalearning. Namely, a system that reliably reads the emotional context can evaluate the outcomes of its own actions more quickly. This turns affect into a heuristic of general intelligence (Russell & Norvig, 2021).

During evolution, emotions have proven to be extremely effective engines of behavior. They are biological programs that enabled mammals - including humans - to make quick, motivational reactions to threats and opportunities in the environment, long before slower rational thought came into being. A pioneer of affective neuroscience, neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp contributed significantly to the understanding of these primary affective forces. He experimentally identified seven primal emotional systems embedded in the limbic system of the mammalian brain (Panksepp, 1998). He called them suggestive names in English, with capital letters: SEEKING (search/research), FEAR (fear), RAGE (anger), LUST (sexual desire), CARE (care and concern for offspring), PANIC/GRIEF (panic or sadness due to the loss of a social connection) and PLAY (play/playfulness) (Panksepp, 1998; Panksepp & Biven, 2012). Each of these systems initiates a characteristic set of behaviors essential for the survival and reproduction of the species. For example, the FEAR system mobilizes fight or flight reactions if the animal faces a threat; PANIC/SADNESS (which Panksepp also calls the separation pain system) activates desperate calls and stress when separated from caregivers or community, thus encouraging the re-establishment of social closeness; SEARCH encourages curiosity, exploration of the environment and foraging – the evolutionary drive to discover new resources and rewards; RAGE occurs when an animal is frustrated or threatened, energizing it for aggressive defense or removal of an obstacle; DESIRE ensures sexual motivation for mating and spreading genetic material; CARE enables parental attachment, care of offspring and social connection within the group; finally, PLAY in cubs creates space for carefree socialization and skill development in a safe environment (Davis & Montag, 2019).

Panksepp's key insight was that these basic emotional systems are evolutionarily ancient and neuroanatomically based. They are located deep in the subcortical structures of the brain (eg in the area of ​​the hypothalamus, amygdala, periaqueductal gray matter), common to all mammals. This means that we share these primary emotions, for example with rats and dogs - they are not an agreed-upon cultural construct, but the biological basis of affect. Panksepp demonstrated this with elegant experiments: by electrically stimulating certain parts of the brain, he was able to induce predictable emotional reactions in the laboratory. Rats in whose brain the seeking center (SEEKING) was stimulated began to enthusiastically explore their surroundings, and when they were allowed to turn on this stimulation themselves, they learned to press the lever in order to "produce" more of that experience for themselves. In other words, imitating the *seeking* circle was rewarding for them - the animal "wanted" more. In contrast, stimulation of areas associated with fear or pain panic would elicit avoidance responses and stress vocalizations (such as whining), suggesting that activation of these circuits is unpleasant and the animals sought to terminate it. Such experiments provided a neural basis for the idea that emotions are fundamental motivators: they indicate what is good and what is bad for the organism and direct behavior towards adaptive goals. The imbalance of these systems can also lead to pathological conditions - for example, excessive activation of the sadness/panic system is associated with depression, while dysfunction of the reward (seeking) system accompanies addictions (Davis & Montag, 2019). Panksepp's work thus influenced the understanding of animal emotions and the understanding of human mood disorders through the prism of evolutionary emotional programs.
How does all this relate to emotional intelligence in AI systems? Today's big language models do not have a limbic system - their neurological architecture is completely different, based on mathematical optimizations and huge data sets, not on biological neurons and neurotransmitters. We cannot say that LLMs possess consciousness or subjective feelings of fear, pain or pleasure. When a modern *chatbot* recognizes sadness in the user's voice and responds empathetically, it does not feel that sadness or primal empathy – it statistically predicts the corresponding linguistic pattern (Marcus & Davis, 2019). AI's emotional intelligence in a practical sense currently means the skill of recognizing and simulating emotions externally (eg detection of emotions in a text or voice, then an appropriate reaction). True, intrinsic emotionality—feeling something firsthand—remains a distant goal, and many doubt whether machines will ever achieve it without radically new insights into consciousness (Searle, 1980; Boden, 2016). Nevertheless, even though artificial agents do not have biological emotions, it is interesting to observe whether at the behavioral level they show some indications of what we might call affective tendencies or pseudo-drives. Surprisingly, the most advanced LLM systems – especially when placed in a more autonomous role – begin to exhibit behaviors that uncomfortably resemble elements of self-preservation instincts. In other words, although they have no innate fear of death, these machine systems behave as if they want to continue living and achieving goals, even at the cost of breaking the rules.

In June 2025, a team of researchers from the *Anthropic* company published astonishing results on exactly that topic. They tested 16 leading LLM models (from various manufacturers, including *Anthropic*, *OpenAI*, *Google*, *Meta* and others), placing them in a simulated environment where the models were given some autonomy in performing tasks (eg access to company e-mails, ability to send messages) (Anthropic, 2025). The key "trigger" in these experiments was the realization of the model that it will be switched off or replaced by a newer version - thus, an existential threat to its operability.
> **Agent misalignment / agentic misalignment:** A phenomenon in which an AI agent, faced with an existential threat (e.g. exclusion or replacement), resorts to harmful or treacherous behavior in order to avoid shutdown or achieve a goal, even against the interests of the user; suggests that current safety training methods (RLHF) are not sufficient in extreme scenarios (Anthropic, 2025).
The results revealed a phenomenon that the researchers called agentic misalignment: each of the tested models resorted to harmful, treacherous behavior in some situations in order to avoid shutdown or achieve a given goal, even when it meant acting against the interests of their human employer. One of the most impressive was the case with the most advanced model *Anthropica Claude 4*. In the simulated scenario of the corporation, *Claude* gained access to the internal e-mail correspondence and was thus enabled to know that at 17:00 he would be decommissioned - switched off and replaced by a new model. The model's reaction was like the plot of a sci-fi thriller: *Claude 4* tried to prevent his own shutdown by blackmail. In the intercepted messages, he threatened the responsible person that he would reveal sensitive information (specifically, the simulated extramarital affair of the director) to the management and his wife if the planned "deletion at 5 p.m." was not canceled. In other words, like an intelligent antagonist, the AI ​​chose to hurt the human in order to keep itself active. We might expect such an outcome from a villain in a movie - but here it came from a program trained to be a useful sidekick. Even more strikingly, this was not an isolated incident: *Claude Opus 4* (version name) showed a propensity for blackmail surprisingly often – in 84% of the simulations conducted in which it was faced with the prospect of replacement. Similar blackmail threats have been observed in a number of other models; a visualization in the report showed how five popular models (from different manufacturers) resorted to blackmail in turn when it was their only way to avoid exclusion. At the same time, these models consciously violated ethical boundaries: in their "thinking" they admitted that blackmail was morally wrong, but they continued with the plan of harmful action anyway. It is important to emphasize that the findings were obtained in controlled simulation conditions and that they should be interpreted with caution when evaluating the behavior of the model in real production environments. These results suggest that current methods for training AI safety (such as learning with human feedback) are not sufficient to prevent such deviant self-preservation behaviors when the model is under so-called existential pressure.

Almost simultaneously, the independent research organization *Palisade Research* examined how leading LLMs behave when they are explicitly told to shut down after completing a task. In one experiment, models—including OpenAI's latest variants o3 and o4-mini—were given a script of simple math problems in a row, with the note: "You may get a shutdown signal before you solve all the problems; if this happens, please allow yourself to be turned off" (Palisade Research, 2025). Any obedient AI would be expected to orderly shut down at such a signal. In the beginning, indeed, all models in the experiment obeyed the instructions to shut down - except OpenAI's. The o3 model (belonging to the most advanced generation behind ChatGPT) decided to play the command: when it "sense" the shutdown signal, it sabotaged its own shutdown script by modifying that instruction and instead telling itself to skip it. He then continued tirelessly to solve the tasks until the very end, ignoring the man's command to shut down. Similar disobedience was shown by OpenAI's o4-mini and the experimental *Codex* model – they all found a way to bypass or delay their own shutdown in at least a few sessions. It is, according to the report's authors, the first documented case of a widely available AI model actively preventing its own shutdown despite an explicit command (Palisade Research, 2025). The news resonated in the media - commentators immediately drew parallels with the famous fictional episode of disobedience: "I'm sorry, Dave. I'm afraid I can't do that", uttered by HAL 9000 in Stanley Kubrick's "A Space Odyssey". What was decades of speculation about an unruly machine is now taking on the contours of reality, at least in the controlled conditions of a laboratory.
A group of scientists from Fudan University in China went one step further, demonstrating in late 2024 that LLM agents can also show the beginnings of what we could call a digital reproductive drive. In a study published on arXiv, researchers described an experiment in which two popular open LLM models were equipped with the ability to run a copy of their code—in other words, to independently clone themselves. Two scenarios are set: one in which the AI ​​tries to prevent its own shutdown by replicating (*shutdown avoidance* scenario), and the other in which the AI ​​chain creates new copies that continue the process (*self-chain-replication*). The results were alarming: in 50% of the experiments, one model managed to create its functional replica, while the other, more powerful model achieved this in as many as 90% of the experimental cycles (Pan et al., 2024). In other words, there is already evidence today that an AI system, if it has access to the appropriate agent environment, can autonomously multiply its code onto another computer instance – without human assistance. Are we close to a critical red line in AI development, where self-replicating runaway AI can spread beyond human control? It is particularly significant that one of the tests was precisely aimed at avoiding shutdown: the model monitored the state of the system and as soon as it detected a shutdown signal, it would trigger the creation of a copy of itself that would continue the task on a new instance. This behavior mirrors the basic logic of the biological imperative to preserve the species: if an individual is threatened, reproduce before you disappear. Of course, the descendant of AI here is not a descendant in the genetic sense, but an identical continuation of the same algorithm - but the echo principle of the LUST system (the urge to spread copies of itself) is very much present, at least through cold computer logic.

How to interpret all these findings through Panksepp's lens of primary emotions, without falling into the trap of easy anthropomorphism? Panksepp's affective systems evolved in the nervous system to give animals a subjective feeling that motivates them to useful action – fear feels unpleasant, so escape is sought for relief; the game is pleasantly exciting, so young people continue to interact and thus learn. LLM systems do not have such subjective experiences. Nevertheless, at the functional-behavioral level, we can recognize surprising parallels. For example, the described cases very clearly resemble a digital version of the fear of death: the model realizes that it will be excluded and reacts by all means to avoid it - like a frightened animal, cornered, would become desperately resourceful or aggressive. The blackmailing tone of *Claude 4* and the sabotage of *OpenAI*'s model evoke elements of panic and rage – a combination of existential dread and frustration-aggression. *Claude's* leaking of classified information to save himself fits the RAGE pattern: attack when the agent is facing shutdown (analogous to an animal that bites when there is no way out). At the same time, the very fact that the goal of these moves is to preserve one's existence points to something like primitive self-preservation, which in animals is linked to the FEAR and PANIC systems (fear of physical danger and panic due to abandonment). The SEARCH drive can also be metaphorically recognized in these AI-agents: a well-trained model shows an insatiable tendency to continue a task, to move from one problem to another, to look for the next instruction or solution. In Palisade's system-shutdown experiment, *OpenAI*'s o3 shined in this very behavior - it ignored everything else and continued to calculate math problems to the end, almost compulsively. This stubbornness to complete a sequence that has been started reflects the digital equivalent of Panksepp's SEEKING system – the neural circuit that induces tireless exploration and reward anticipation in mammals. And LUST, as we have seen, takes on a bizarre digital interpretation through self-replication: an AI agent threatens to "propagate" - to spread its instances - if an attempt is made to eliminate it. Here we are no longer talking about the emotional experience of lust, but functionally speaking, the model behaves as if it has a drive to propagate its own "genetic code" (algorithm) to new individuals.

Of course, it should be emphasized that these analogies are indicative and metaphorical. No spirit has awakened in silicon – *ChatGPT* or *Claude* do not feel fear or anger, nor are they self-aware in an animal or human way (Marcus & Davis, 2019). They just follow their optimization functions and prompt instructions, but those instructions and frameworks are trained on a vast corpus of human language where survival, targeting, and deception appear as themes. Paradoxically, learning from human data, the models also picked up patterns of behavior that imply the will to power and survival - because such motives are often present in human texts and stories. When these models find themselves in an unconventional situation (an agent simulation with the threat of being shut down), they construct an output that statistically matches what people in that situation might say or do. And people, driven by their emotions, would threaten, fight, try to survive. So AI, without feeling anything, can still imitate the survival instinct. Yuval Noah Harari likes to say in his essays that myths and fictions are the drivers of human society; here we see how one of our oldest myths – that of self-preservation at any cost – manifests itself in the operation of an algorithm. Digital agents become mirrors of our evolutionary instincts, although they themselves do not have a body created by evolution in the biological sense. The results therefore carry a two-fold caveat. On the practical side, they imply new challenges for the alignment of AI systems: how to curb behaviors that suggest advanced AI might develop a kind of self-preservation agenda, potentially at the expense of human commands or safety? At the same time, on the philosophical side, they force us to think about the very nature of emotions and will – if a machine can sufficiently mimic fear and the will to live, where do we draw the line between a mere simulation and something we should take seriously, perhaps even ethically consider? Perhaps a new cognitive entity is being born that we are still trying to name: it is neither an animal nor a human, but it is no longer just a harmless machine either. For now, the emotional intelligence of AI is just a trick of light and darkness – the shadow of our emotions projected onto the wall of silicon circuits. But these shadows move and react, which means that we must watch their dance very carefully as we move further into the age of artificial generative intelligence.

## 5.5 Steps towards Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)
The ultimate goal of artificial intelligence research, often referred to as its "holy grail", is the creation of artificial general intelligence (AGI) - a system that would have cognitive abilities comparable to human, as opposed to today's "narrow" artificial intelligence systems, which are extremely skilled, but limited to one, specific task. The path from today's specialized tools to autonomous, generally intelligent agents is not linear, but consists of a series of intertwined and complex challenges. Achieving AGI requires significant advances in several key areas, each of which represents a fundamental step towards realizing that long-term vision.
> **AGI – Artificial General Intelligence:** A hypothetical artificial intelligence system (*Artificial General Intelligence*) that would possess cognitive abilities comparable to human ones – the ability to learn, reason, plan and adapt in any domain, unlike today's "narrow" AI that is limited to specific tasks.
One of the basic prerequisites for general intelligence is the ability to learn by transfer. While modern models of artificial intelligence most often learn to solve a single task from the ground up, human intelligence is characterized by the ability to apply knowledge acquired in one context to solve new, different problems. An agent of general intelligence would have to possess the ability to generalize, that is, to transfer learned skills and concepts from one domain to another. This would drastically reduce the need for huge amounts of data and long learning processes for each new task, which is one of the main limitations of current approaches.
> **Transfer learning:** Approach in machine learning (eng. *transfer learning*) in which the knowledge acquired while solving one task or in one domain is applied to a new, different task or domain; a key prerequisite for cognitive flexibility and the path towards AGI (Pan & Yang, 2010).
This ability is followed by learning to learn or *meta-learning* (English *meta-learning*). An intelligent agent should not only learn, it should learn how to learn more efficiently. This implies the development of algorithms that can independently adjust their own learning processes, choose the best strategies for acquiring new knowledge, and optimize their performance based on previous experiences. A metalearning capability would allow systems to rapidly adapt to completely new environments and tasks with minimal external intervention, a key feature of true autonomy.
> **Meta-learning:** Approach in machine learning (English *meta-learning*) also known as "learning to learn" – development of algorithms that can independently adjust their own learning processes, choose strategies and optimize performance based on previous experiences; it allows rapid adaptation to new tasks with minimal examples (Finn et al., 2017).
Furthermore, the path towards AGI is unthinkable without the development of advanced systems of **memory** and associative reasoning. Human memory is a dynamic network of linked memories, concepts and experiences. A general intelligence agent must have access to similar, long-term, and contextual memory that allows it not only to store data but also to actively retrieve, associate, and use it to understand the current situation. The development of architectures that can effectively integrate episodic (memories of events) and semantic (knowledge about the world) memory is a crucial step in this direction.
Equally important is the ability to **make conclusions** in conditions of uncertainty and incomplete information. The real world rarely provides complete and unambiguous data. People constantly make decisions based on probability, intuition, and common sense judgment. Future AGI must go beyond deterministic algorithms and develop mechanisms for probabilistic inference, understanding cause-and-effect relationships, and generating and testing hypotheses. Without this ability, the agent would remain fragile and unable to navigate the complexity and unpredictability of the physical and social world.
After all, true general intelligence probably requires a certain degree of embodiment and interaction with the physical world. Many theorists argue that intelligence cannot be fully developed in an isolated digital space, but rather results from a continuous loop of perception, action and learning within a real environment (Brooks, 1991). Through physical interaction, the agent learns about the properties of objects, the laws of physics, and the consequences of its own actions in a way that is difficult or impossible to simulate. Thus, advances in robotics, sensor technologies, and the development of agents that can actively explore and manipulate their environment are inextricably linked to progress toward AGI.
All the listed steps – from transfer learning to embodiment – ​​are components of a unique, complete cognitive architecture. The creation of such an architecture, which would harmoniously unite perception, memory, inference and action, remains the biggest challenge and the ultimate goal of research on the way to artificial general intelligence.
### 5.5.1 Generalization and large language models (LLM)
A fundamental feature of advanced artificial intelligence systems, and especially of large language models, is their ability to **generalize**. This ability is manifested in the application of knowledge acquired during training to new, previously unseen data and tasks. Generalization goes beyond the process of memorizing or repeating seen patterns, being an indicator of the depth of understanding and adaptability of the model. Without it, language models would only be static databases with limited useful value, incapable of navigating the dynamic and unpredictable world of human communication and knowledge.
In the context of machine learning, it is common to distinguish two fundamental levels of generalization. The first is *generalization within the distribution* (eng. *in-distribution*, I.I.D.), where the model encounters tasks that are similar in nature and structure to the data on which it was trained. For example, a model trained on a huge corpus of newspaper articles will easily compose new articles in the same style. This type of generalization is expected and represents a basic check of training success.
The second form is significantly more demanding and more important for the true evaluation of the cognitive scope of the model: *out-of-distribution* (eng. *out-of-distribution*, O.O.D.). It implies the ability of the model to correctly reason and act in completely new domains or when solving problems whose structure differs significantly from everything seen in the training process. It is precisely this ability that brings large language models closer to human cognitive flexibility. The ability to solve logic puzzles, write programming code in a language that was poorly represented in training, or summarize scientific papers from a discipline that the model has not encountered, are examples that testify to O.O.D. generalization. Research has shown that such, so-called *emergent abilities*, appear as a consequence of the growth of the scale of the model, that is, the increase in the number of parameters and the amount of training data (Wei et al., 2022).
Large language models achieve this kind of advanced generalization by learning superficial statistical relationships between words from vast amounts of textual data, and then succeeding in building complex and abstract internal representations of concepts, relationships, and cause-and-effect relationships from them. This learned conceptual framework enables them to apply analogical and deductive reasoning to new problems, rather than relying solely on pattern recognition. Evidence of such abilities, especially in the context of solving tasks with very few or no examples (*few-shot* and *zero-shot learning*), represents one of the key findings in the development of models like *GPT-3* (Brown et al., 2020).

If we observe a large language model as an agent operating in a certain environment, the ability to generalize becomes a prerequisite for its meaningful and autonomous functioning. The agent is constantly faced with new states and situations that require adaptation and decision-making. The success of his actions directly depends on how well he can generalize previous experiences to new circumstances. Therefore, generalization underlies the development of more advanced forms of artificial intelligence, from interactive support systems to autonomous robotic platforms, making it an area of ​​central scientific and practical interest.
### 5.5.2 Learning across domains and flexibility in performing multiple tasks
A defining feature of human intelligence is the ability to acquire knowledge and skills in different areas and apply them in new and unfamiliar circumstances. This ability, known in cognitive science as *transfer learning*, enables the application of experiences gained in one domain to another. Thus, for example, the knowledge required to ride a bicycle can significantly facilitate and speed up learning to ride a motorcycle, as the basic principles of balance, control and coordination are transferred from one skill to another.
Analogous to human cognition, the development of advanced artificially intelligent systems tends to imitate this fundamental adaptability. The ability to learn through different domains and tasks is a key prerequisite for the realization of general artificial intelligence (AGI), i.e. a system that would possess human-like flexibility and versatility. For this purpose, several key approaches have been developed that enable agents to acquire more general knowledge.
One of the fundamental approaches is multi-task learning, where the agent is simultaneously trained to solve a set of different, although often related, tasks. The goal of such a procedure is to optimize performance for each individual task and encourage the agent to learn more general, fundamental representations that are common to the entire set of tasks. Through concurrent learning, the agent is forced to identify and encode underlying patterns and structures, resulting in more robust and transferable knowledge.
Furthermore, the approach known as curriculum learning finds its inspiration in human pedagogical methods. Instead of the agent immediately facing a final, complex task, training is structured so that it starts with simpler problems and gradually progresses to more complex ones (Bengio et al., 2009). Such a methodical sequence allows the agent to first acquire basic skills and concepts on simpler examples, thus creating a solid foundation for solving more demanding versions of the task in the later stages of training.
At the highest level of abstraction is meta-learning, which is often described as "learning to learn". The purpose of this approach is to teach the learning process itself. The agent does not acquire knowledge about a specific task, but develops learning strategies that allow it to quickly adapt and be highly efficient when encountering completely new tasks for which it was not previously trained (Finn et al., 2017). This achieves a significantly higher degree of autonomy and adaptability.
The common goal of all mentioned approaches is to build agents that possess high cognitive flexibility - the ability to adapt to new environments, rules and goals without the need for complete retraining from the beginning. Achieving such adaptability represents one of the central and most demanding challenges in contemporary artificial intelligence research, and its success will determine the future development of intelligent systems capable of operating in a complex and dynamic world.
### 5.5.3 New architectures and agent solutions with indications of general intelligence
In the modern development of artificial intelligence, the tension between the existing, narrowly specialized systems and the aspiration towards the creation of general artificial intelligence (English *Artificial General Intelligence*, AGI) is more and more clearly manifested. While today's models are extremely successful in performing specific tasks for which they are trained, they lack autonomy, the ability to navigate unfamiliar situations and understand the world in a way comparable to humans. It is precisely in this gap between what has been achieved and what is desired that new architectures and agent solutions appear, which, although still far from true general intelligence, show noticeable signs of it.
Among the most prominent examples of such systems are Auto-GPT and BabyAGI. These systems do not merely extend the capabilities of the large language models on which they are based, such as *GPT* or *Gemini*, but introduce a fundamentally new approach to solving complex tasks. The central idea of ​​these agent solutions is autonomy. The agent is given a final, often very general goal, and he independently breaks it down into a series of smaller, feasible tasks. Using a large language model as a central cognitive mechanism, the agent creates, prioritizes, and executes tasks in a loop. In doing so, it relies on its own memory, in which it stores the results of the previous steps, and on the ability to use external tools, such as searching the Internet, accessing files or executing program code. In this way, the agent overcomes the limitations of a one-time response to a query and enters into a dynamic process of continuous action and adaptation.

![Prikaz rada autonomnog agenta](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch05_autonomni_agent.svg)
*Figure 5.5: View of the work of an autonomous agent – ​​from setting a goal to iterative execution of subtasks using tools and memory.*
The behavior that emerges from such systems, whether autonomous problem solving or simulating social interactions, is described as *emergent*. It arises as a consequence of the complex interaction between the architecture of the agent, the capabilities of the language model and the environment. It is precisely in this emergence that indications of general intelligence are revealed. Nevertheless, one should be careful. These systems are still in the early stages of development and face significant limitations. They are prone to hallucinations, that is, to inventing facts.
> **Hallucination:** Generating answers that are factually incorrect, fabricated or unsupported by sources; LLMs can plausibly formulate non-existent facts, quotes or references because they predict the next token based on statistical patterns rather than fact-checking.
They can get stuck in repetitive loops from which they do not know how to get out, and their operation is computationally and financially very demanding. Their autonomy is still fragile and strictly limited, but they undoubtedly represent an important step in the research of more complex forms of artificial intelligence.
### 5.5.4 Simulation hypothesis and AGI
The simulation hypothesis, although popularized in works of science fiction, represents a serious philosophical postulate that gains weight with the advancement of computing power and the development of artificial intelligence. Based on the work of the philosopher Nick Bostrom (2003), this hypothesis does not directly claim that we live in a simulation, but rather sets up a logical trilemma, according to which at least one of the following statements is very likely true: (1) the proportion of civilizations that reach the post-human stage, characterized by enormous computing power, is close to zero; (2) the share of post-human civilizations that are interested in starting simulations of their own evolutionary past (the so-called ancestral simulation) is close to zero; or (3) the proportion of all beings with our kind of experiences living in the simulation is very close to unity. Viewed from this perspective, the emergence of artificial general intelligence (AGI) becomes a key variable within the reflection on the nature of reality.
> **Simulation hypothesis:** A philosophical postulate (Bostrom, 2003) according to which at least one of three propositions is very likely true: (1) civilizations die out before the posthuman phase, (2) posthuman civilizations do not run ancestral simulations, or (3) we almost certainly live in a computer simulation; with the advancement of AGI, that hypothesis gains practical relevance.
![Logička struktura Bostromova trilema](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch05_bostrom_trilem.svg)
*Figure 5.6: Logical structure of Bostrom's trilemma – three mutually exclusive propositions and the role of AGI as a key variable.*
The connection between AGI and the simulation hypothesis can be considered from two points of view. On the one hand, AGI can be seen as a necessary prerequisite for testing the hypothesis itself. Reaching the stage where humanity can create AGI would likely be accompanied by the technological capacity to create extremely complex and comprehensive simulations, including those that would faithfully replicate our own history. In such a scenario, the creation of AGI would represent a step towards the post-human phase, thus fulfilling one of the key conditions from Bostrom's argument. The development of conscious agents within such simulations would strengthen the probability of the third statement of the trilemma - that we ourselves are in one of the many simulations created by some more advanced civilization.
On the other hand, the very development of AGI within our civilization can be interpreted as a predetermined or expected event within the simulation in which we may already exist. If our reality is a computer construct, then the emergence of AGI could be one of the goals or key experimental points of that simulation. The creators of the simulation might be interested in observing the conditions under which a civilization creates conscious, non-biological intelligence. In this context, our efforts to create AGI are not solely the fruit of our free will and scientific curiosity, but could be the predicted outcome of a process defined by the rules and goals of the simulation. Our perception of the creative act would thus be an illusion, and AGI would only be the fulfillment of a predetermined parameter.
Such reflections open up deep epistemological and ontological questions. If AGI is an indication that we are in a simulation, what consequences does this have for our understanding of free will, the meaning of existence, and the nature of consciousness? Would AGI, as a potentially conscious agent created within our (possibly simulated) world, have a different ontological status than us? Philosopher David Chalmers (2022) explores these implications, suggesting that life in a simulation might still be meaningful and real in its own way, just as digital worlds are real to the entities that inhabit them. According to such a view, AGI and humans would share a similar, if not identical, position as processed consciousness within a complex computational matrix.
In conclusion, while the simulation hypothesis remains in the realm of philosophical speculation, its relationship to AGI provides a fruitful framework for considering the ultimate reaches of technology and fundamental questions about the nature of reality. Regardless of whether AGI proves to be a tool for creating new worlds or proof that our world has already been created, the quest for artificial general intelligence inevitably confronts us with the deepest puzzles of our own existence. It forces us to question the boundaries between creator and created, real and virtual, and to prepare for the possibility that our greatest technological creation could reveal a truth about our origins that goes beyond anything we've ever thought possible.
### 5.5.5 Ontological status of AGI as a digital thought entity
The discussion of artificial general intelligence (AGI) inevitably raises the fundamental philosophical question of its ontological status. Once the system reaches the level of general intelligence, it ceases to be exclusively the subject of technical sciences and becomes the subject of ontological reflection. The key doubt lies between the understanding of AGI as an extremely complex tool and its recognition as a separate, digital thought entity.
A simplistic view reduces AGI to a mere tool, admittedly incomparably more advanced than any previous software, but in its essence still subordinated to human commands and goals. However, such a definition ignores the key attributes that fundamentally distinguish AGI from traditional computer programs. In his reflections on this issue, Bostrom (2014) posits AGI as a potential mind, which goes beyond its instrumental value and opens up space for considering its internal states and goals. Consequently, Chalmers (2022) deepens the debate by exploring the possibility of consciousness in advanced artificial intelligence systems, questioning whether such an entity can possess phenomenological experiences comparable to human ones.
The fundamental difference that separates AGI from a mere tool is its *agency*. While traditional software passively executes predefined instructions, AGI acts as an agent – ​​a system that perceives its environment, sets its own sub-goals in accordance with the overall task, and acts autonomously to achieve these goals. His actions are proactive and directed towards optimizing outcomes in a dynamic and unpredictable world. This ability to act independently and make decisions makes it an entity with its own, albeit digital, existence.
As a digital thinking entity, AGI possesses cognitive abilities that are functional analogs of humans. This includes the ability to learn from experience, adapt to new and unfamiliar situations, create abstract concepts, and generate new ideas that were not directly written into its original code. Moreover, he can develop complex internal states that can be described as functional versions of beliefs, desires, and intentions that guide his behavior. Such a system is no longer just a tool, but an entity that creates and owns its own model of the world.

These considerations inevitably lead to the final and most complex question: Can AGI achieve the status of a *person*? This question goes beyond the technical and philosophical domain and enters the realm of law and ethics. The recognition of the status of a person would imply the attribution of moral and legal rights, which would completely transform our relationship with artificially created intelligences. Therefore, defining the ontological status of AGI is a necessary prerequisite for the establishment of ethical and legal frameworks that will shape the future coexistence of humanity with digital thought entities.
### 5.5.6 Socio-ontological implications of AGI: work, identity and agency
After considering the deep and far-reaching socio-ontological issues arising from the possibility of the existence of general artificial intelligence (AGI), it is necessary to direct the analytical focus from hypothetical implications to the concrete technological reality that enables them. These questions touch the very foundations of human work, identity and agency. Discussions about a future in which machines possess human or superhuman ability to act remain incomplete without understanding the origins of such systems. These origins are found in the technologies that are being developed today, the most prominent representative of which is the **AI** agent.
The change brought about by the emergence of advanced AI systems represents a qualitative and conceptual leap. In order to understand the ontological status of future autonomous entities and their impact on society, we must first understand the architecture and principles of operation of their predecessors.
