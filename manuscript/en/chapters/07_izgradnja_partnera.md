# 7. Building communication partners: examples
After in the previous chapter we considered the theoretical foundations and determined the key components that enable the "birth" of a communication partner, we now move from abstract principles to their concrete application. This chapter is devoted to the practical dimension of building such agents, where theoretical models meet the challenges of real implementation. We will observe how theoretical assumptions about context, learning and adaptation translate into the architecture of real systems, shaping their behavior and interaction capabilities.
The purpose of this chapter is twofold. First, the goal is to show the diversity of approaches in building communication partners through carefully selected examples from different domains - from customer support and education to personal assistants. Second, through in-depth case studies, we will explore specific problems, successful solutions, and trade-offs faced by engineers and designers. By analyzing these examples, we will discover how the general principles are manifested in practice and what unforeseen difficulties and possibilities emerge during development and use. Thus, theoretical understanding is enriched with insights gained from experience, offering a more complete picture of what is needed to successfully build and maintain functional and reliable communication partners.

## 7.1 Digital interlocutors: chatbots and virtual assistants in the age of LLMs
Digital interlocutors, embodied in chatbots and virtual assistants, represent one of the most prominent human-computer interaction interfaces in the modern technological landscape. Their rapid development and ubiquitous application are inextricably linked to progress in the field of large language models, which fundamentally transformed their capabilities and roles. While chatbots and virtual assistants have traditionally been seen as two relatively separate categories, recent technological advances are erasing those boundaries and introducing a new paradigm of intelligent, autonomous systems.

### Definition of concepts: from scripted answers to complex tasks
Historically, the fundamental difference between a chatbot and a virtual assistant rested on the degree of complexity and breadth of functionality. *Chatbots* were originally conceived as simpler programs, often based on predefined rules or scripts. Their primary purpose was to conduct highly specialized conversations within a specific domain, such as providing customer support for a specific product, answering frequently asked questions, or collecting basic data from users.

> **Chatbot:** A program for conducting conversations with the user, originally based on rules or scripts; modern chatbots often use LLM and can conduct complex, context-aware dialogues.
> **Virtual Assistant:** A more complex digital interlocutor deeply integrated with the device and external services, capable of a wider range of tasks (calendar management, smart home, search, messages); the line towards chatbot is blurring with the advent of LLMs.

On the other hand, virtual assistants, such as Amazon's *Alexa*, Apple's *Siri* or Google's *Assistant*, were designed from the beginning as much more complex systems. Their fundamental feature is deep integration with the operating system of the device and a wide ecosystem of external services and applications. Such integration allows them to perform diverse and complex tasks that go beyond mere conversation - from managing a smart home and setting reminders to sending messages, searching the Internet and interacting with other software tools.
However, the emergence and commercialization of large language models initiated a radical transformation. The ability of LLMs to understand context, generate coherent and creative text, and reason about complex problems has enabled the new generation of *chatbots* to acquire, and even surpass, the capabilities of traditional virtual assistants. Modern *chatbots*, powered by technologies such as those developed by OpenAI (2023), are no longer limited to rigid scripts. They can have fluid, context-aware conversations, condense documents, write programming code, and perform tasks that require multi-level planning and reasoning. This blurs the once clear dividing line between the two concepts, and a new, more comprehensive concept - the concept of an agent - enters the spotlight.

### The rise of autonomous agents
The term agent in the context of artificial intelligence means a system that can independently perceive its environment and act autonomously to achieve set goals. The classic definition describes an agent as an entity that perceives the environment through sensors and influences it through actuators (Russell & Norvig, 2021). In the digital world, "sensors" can be API calls that retrieve information from the Internet or read files from disk, while "actuators" can be tools to send email, execute program code, or interact with other software interfaces.

Large language models act as the central "brain" or cognitive core of such agents. They give them the ability to reason, plan and break down complex goals into a series of smaller, actionable steps. When LLM is granted access to external tools, it outgrows the role of a passive text generator and becomes a proactive task executor. Such a system goes beyond the traditional concept of a chatbot and takes on the characteristics of an autonomous agent. Examples such as the Auto-GPT or AgentGPT projects illustrate this shift: the user sets a general and complex goal (e.g. "research the electric car market and compile a report on key competitors"), and the agent independently forms a plan, searches the Internet, analyzes the collected data, uses tools to process it, and finally generates the required report, while learning and adapting its actions without constant human intervention.
This transition from reactive interlocutors to proactive agents represents a fundamental paradigm shift, opening the door to applications that until recently belonged to the domain of science fiction and posing new challenges in the field of security, ethics and management of autonomous systems.

## 7.2 Perspective-changing applications: where do agents create value?
The theoretical potential of autonomous agents, although extremely stimulating, gains its full meaning only with an insight into real applications where their capabilities bring measurable and transformative value. Considering agents solely as advanced tools for executing individual commands is insufficient; their true strength is manifested in the ability to independently orchestrate complex workflows, which opens up new horizons in problem solving and value creation. In the following, the key areas in which agents are already showing the ability to fundamentally change established practices are explained in more detail.

### Automation of complex research and analytical processes
One of the most fruitful areas of application of agents is the automation of time-consuming and complex research tasks. Let's imagine an agent entrusted with the task of creating a detailed report on a specific topic, for example the impact of climate change on agriculture in the Mediterranean. Such an agent does not perform its task with a single query, but starts a complete process. First, it independently formulates a series of relevant queries to search scientific databases, newspaper archives and official reports. Then, it systematically collects, filters and evaluates the credibility of the found sources. The key step that follows is synthesis – the agent analyzes the collected information, recognizes patterns, contradictions and key arguments and structures them into a coherent narrative. The final product is a complete report that includes sets of links or summaries complete with neat citations and references according to relevant academic standards. In this way, tasks that would take a human researcher days or weeks of work are completed in a much shorter time, with a high level of precision.

### Development of program code and software solutions
In the domain of software engineering, the role of agents is also slowly going beyond the function of mere code assistants. Autonomous agents are capable of taking over entire development cycles. Starting from the analysis of the user's requirements, specified in natural language, the agent can create an architectural design of the application, select suitable technological frameworks, and then write, test and correct (*debug*) the necessary program code. Their ability is not limited to generating code snippets; they can independently manage repositories, solve dependencies, write unit tests and even run automated deployment processes on servers. This approach allows development teams to focus on the strategic and creative aspects of development, while agents take on repetitive and technically demanding but well-defined tasks. This speeds up the entire software development process and makes it more resistant to human errors. Examples of such systems are helpers for writing code such as Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Windsurf and others.

### Personalized customer interaction and advanced support
Customer support systems, which have traditionally relied on simple *chatbots* with pre-defined scenarios, are undergoing a profound transformation. Agents bring the ability to conduct complex, multi-step dialogues where they remember the context of the conversation, access the history of user interactions, and dynamically use external tools to solve a specific problem. For example, a user reporting a product delivery problem can interact with an agent who will independently check the order status in the logistics system, communicate with the delivery service via their API and offer the user a specific solution, such as a new delivery date or a refund. Equally, in the role of personal assistants, agents can manage calendars, book trips and meetings, taking into account complex user preferences and schedule overlaps, which represents a significant step forward compared to existing digital assistants.

### Strategic management, marketing and business analysis
In a business environment, agents become a powerful decision-making tool. It is possible to assign them the tasks of continuous monitoring of market trends, analysis of competitor activities or monitoring of public sentiment on social networks. An agent in charge of optimizing a marketing campaign can autonomously analyze data on the success of ads on different platforms, allocate budget according to those channels that bring the best return on investment, and even independently generate and test new creative versions of ads. In finance, agents can perform complex analyzes of financial statements, identify risks or anomalies, and prepare summary bases for making strategic decisions. Their value lies in the ability to process and connect vast amounts of unstructured data, identify signals in the noise of information and turn them into concrete, actionable insights, freeing up human experts for tasks of the highest strategic level.

### 7.2.1 Revolution in customer support and marketing
The emergence and application of artificial intelligence means a thorough transformation of the paradigms on which customer support and marketing rested until now. These two, once separate business areas, under the influence of intelligent systems, converge into a single, data-driven function whose goal is to create a complete and deeply personalized user experience. It is a transition from a reactive model, which responds to already existing needs or problems, to a proactive and predictive approach, which anticipates future conditions and acts in accordance with them.
In the domain of customer support, the first and most obvious shift is the automation of the initial points of interaction with users through intelligent agents, i.e. the already mentioned *chatbots* and voice assistants. These systems are trained to immediately and continuously solve a large number of standardized queries, which frees up human resources. Time, as a key resource, is thus diverted from routine tasks to solving complex, multi-layered problems that require human empathy, creativity and critical judgment. However, the reach of artificial intelligence also includes in-depth analysis of a huge amount of unstructured data, such as transcripts of calls, e-mails and messages from social networks.

> **Sentiment analysis:** Automatic analysis of text (transcripts, e-mail, social networks) in order to recognize the emotional tone, attitudes or mood - e.g. frustration, satisfaction, negativity; it is used in customer support to prioritize cases, in marketing to monitor reactions and in public opinion surveys.

By analyzing sentiment from this data, the system can recognize the user's tone, degree of frustration or satisfaction, and based on that determine the priority and urgency of a particular case, providing the human agent with a contextually rich summary before the actual interaction.
In parallel with the transformation of customer support, a revolution in marketing strategies is taking place. The era of mass marketing, whose messages were aimed at broad and poorly defined demographic groups, is leaving, and the period of hyperpersonalization is entering. Machine learning algorithms analyze an individual user's entire interaction history—from browsing patterns and previous purchases to time spent on specific products—to dynamically tailor marketing messages, offers, and the overall user experience to each individual. Recommendation systems, such as those used by platforms such as Netflix or Amazon, are the best-known example of such an approach, where based on the behavior of similar users and individual preferences, it is predicted which content or product is most likely to attract attention (Varian, 2018).

This system, through analysis and prediction, simultaneously generates output values that transform both customer support (proactivity, sentiment analysis) and marketing (hyperpersonalization, predictive offers), thus creating a unique and intelligent customer relationship management cycle.
Furthermore, artificial intelligence systems in marketing rely on the analysis of past interactions, but also enter the field of predictive analytics. I can predict with a high degree of probability the future behavior of users, such as the possibility of abandoning the service (churn prediction*), identify customers with the highest long-term value or assess the optimal moment to send a promotional offer. At the same time, the development of generative models enables the automation of the creation of marketing content. From writing e-mail headlines that encourage opening, to composing posts for social networks adapted to a specific audience, to generating basic versions of advertising texts, artificial intelligence becomes a collaborator in the creative process, significantly speeding up the production and testing of marketing materials (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019).
Artificial intelligence is reshaping the very essence of the relationship between businesses and customers: traditional boundaries between departments are collapsing and a single, intelligent ecosystem is being created where every interaction is recorded, analyzed and used to shape the next step in the customer journey. This achieves the transition from a transactional to a relational business model, where the ultimate goal is to build long-term loyalty through a continuous, relevant and deeply personalized experience.

### 7.2.2 Transformation of education and content personalization
Generative artificial intelligence, especially in the form of autonomous agents, has the potential to make a turning point in the understanding and implementation of educational processes. Its influence goes beyond previous technological innovations that served as aids; it is a radical transformation that moves education from an established, unique model for all students to a completely personalized and dynamic experience. Agents take on the role of personal mentors, adapting to each individual's unique needs, abilities and learning styles.
The central change brought about by AI agents is manifested in the possibility of creating individualized educational curricula. The system begins with an in-depth analysis of existing knowledge, the speed of material acquisition and the cognitive preferences of students. Based on this data, the agent forms a customized curriculum that is not static, but constantly changes and develops in parallel with the progress of the user (Jones & Hrabowski, 2022). If a student demonstrates exceptional understanding of a particular area, the system can guide them toward more advanced topics or, conversely, provide additional materials and exercises for areas where difficulties arise. This abandons the outdated approach in which the entire class progresses at the same pace, regardless of individual differences.
The scope of AI agents extends to the very creation and refinement of educational materials. They are able to generate summaries of complex scientific papers in real time, create quizzes to test knowledge or offer step-by-step explanations of mathematical problems, adapted to the level of understanding of the individual user. At the same time, agents act as excellent curators of knowledge, searching the vast amounts of information available on the Internet to single out the most credible, relevant, and high-quality sources for the student. This dual role, that of creator and that of curator, enables the construction of a rich and multi-layered educational environment.
Learning becomes an active, engaged activity. AI agents can conduct Socratic dialogues, encouraging critical thinking by asking thoughtful questions rather than simply providing answers. They can create complex simulations in which students manage a virtual laboratory, participate in reenactments of historical events, or solve business case studies in a dynamic environment. Such interactive learning models increase knowledge retention and develop problem-solving and analytical reasoning skills that are crucial in the modern world.
This process can be represented as a continuous cycle of interaction between the learner and the AI agent, where each stage builds on the previous one, creating a spiral of knowledge.

The use of intelligent agents also has considerable potential for increasing the availability of education. The systems can provide instant translation of teaching materials into different languages, thus removing language barriers. They can convert text content into speech for visually impaired students or simplify complex sentence structures for people with learning disabilities, such as dyslexia (Rose & Meyer, 2002). In this way, education becomes fairer and more open to all, regardless of their physical, cognitive or geographical circumstances.
Despite the huge advantages, the introduction of AI agents in education also brings with it certain challenges. Excessive dependence on technology could reduce the development of social skills and the ability to explore independently. The issue of privacy and security of student data is of crucial importance, as the systems collect detailed information about their progress and behavior. It is necessary to ensure that algorithms that personalize content do not create "information bubbles" or introduce biases that could limit the learner's exposure to different perspectives. Finally, the role of the human teacher is also changing, becoming more and more a mentor, moderator and emotional support, guiding students through a technology-driven learning process. Its task is to stimulate curiosity and develop those human qualities that a machine cannot replace.

### 7.2.3 Extension of applicability: LLM agents in healthcare, business processes and creative industries
The reach and applicability of language models in the form of autonomous agents go far beyond their initial roles in text processing and generation. Their ability to understand context, plan, and execute complex tasks opens up opportunities for deep integration into diverse areas of human endeavor, reshaping fundamental paradigms in healthcare, business, and creative industries. In each of these sectors, LLM agents represent not only a technological improvement, but a catalyst for fundamental changes in the way of working, deciding and creating.

### Application in healthcare
In the healthcare system, the role of LLM agents goes beyond the function of simple interlocutors or virtual assistants. Their ability to process and analyze vast amounts of unstructured data from electronic health records (EHR) enables them to act as advanced diagnostic collaborators. Based on a synthesis of symptoms, the patient's medical history and the latest scientific knowledge from the medical literature, agents can suggest differential diagnoses and alert doctors to rare diseases or atypical symptom presentations. Their contribution does not stop at diagnosis, but also extends to the creation of personalized treatment plans, taking into account genetic predispositions, lifestyle and specific comorbidities of the patient. However, application in such a sensitive area also opens up complex ethical and technical issues, primarily related to the protection of patient data privacy, in accordance with regulations such as HIPAA (USA) and GDPR (EU), and the imperative of clinical accuracy, where an error can have critical consequences (Chen et al., 2023).

### Optimization of business processes
A similar transformative potential is also evident in the domain of business processes. Automation of routine tasks, such as customer support where agents can conduct complex conversations and resolve inquiries 24 hours a day, represents only the initial level of their integration. A deeper application is seen in the optimization of complex systems such as supply chain management, where agents based on analysis of market trends, logistics data and internal reports can forecast demand, optimize inventory levels and coordinate deliveries autonomously. Furthermore, the ability to automatically generate comprehensive reports from raw business data frees up human resources for strategic decision-making. Therefore, it is not only about mere automation, but about the introduction of a layer of artificial intelligence that enables making more informed and precise business decisions, with a key prerequisite for successful integration with existing ERP and CRM systems (Davenport & Ronanki, 2018).

### Cooperation in creative industries
Finally, the reach of LLM agents extends into the creative industries, an area that has traditionally been considered an exclusively human domain. Here agents assume the role of collaborators or catalysts of the creative process. They are offered to artists, writers, designers and musicians as tools to overcome creative blocks and explore new aesthetic directions. Their application covers a wide range of activities, whether it is generating initial ideas for a script, developing characters, composing musical motifs or creating visual concepts. It is crucial to understand that the goal is not to replace the human creator, but to augment his abilities – providing new starting points and exploring unimagined artistic possibilities. This new paradigm of human-machine cooperation inevitably raises fundamental questions about authorship, originality and ownership of works created in such synergy, opening a new legal and philosophical debate (Epstein et al., 2023).

### 7.2.4 Content personalization paradigm: transversal application of AI algorithms
In today's digital environment, the paradigm of personalization of content is established as a fundamental mechanism of interaction between users and information systems that transforms the ways in which information, products and services are distributed and consumed. At the center of this transformation are artificial intelligence algorithms, whose task is the dynamic modeling of user needs, preferences and behavior with the aim of delivering relevant and timely content. The application of these technologies has an extremely transversal character, permeating different sectors and domains of human activity, from e-commerce and media to education and healthcare.

### Mechanisms and algorithmic bases
The basic principle of personalization rests on a continuous cycle of data collection, its processing and the application of learned patterns. This process begins with recording user interactions with the system – from explicit actions such as rating a product or selecting a movie genre, to implicit signals such as time spent on the page, search history, mouse movements or geographic location. This data forms what is called a *digital footprint* of the user, on the basis of which AI systems build his dynamic profile, i.e. a *digital twin*.
Different classes of algorithms are used to analyze these huge data sets and predict future interests:
**Collaborative Filtering**: This approach is based on the idea of the "wisdom of the crowd". The system recommends content to the user based on the preferences of other, similar users. If user A and user B have shown a similar taste in movies in the past, the system will recommend to user A a movie that user B liked but which A has not yet watched. The strength of this approach lies in the ability to discover unexpected but relevant recommendations (serendipity).
**Content-Based Filtering***: Unlike the collaborative one, this approach is based on the intrinsic features of the contents themselves. If the user often reads articles on quantum physics, the system will recommend new articles containing keywords and concepts related to the field. Attributes such as genre, author, keywords or product technical specifications are analyzed to find those that match the user's existing interest profile.
**Hybrid models:** In practice, hybrid systems are most often applied, which try to combine the advantages of both approaches in order to overcome their individual shortcomings, such as the problem of "cold start" (lack of data for new users or new content) which is characteristic of collaborative filtering (Ricci, Rokach, & Shapira, 2011).
At the heart of this process is the notion of an agent – an autonomous software entity that acts on behalf of a user or system. The AI agent in charge of personalization observes user behavior, learns from it, and independently makes decisions about what content to display, in what form and at what time, with the aim of maximizing relevance and user engagement.

### Transversality of application
The ability of AI algorithms to learn and adapt makes personalization a universally applicable tool. Its transversality is manifested in numerous domains:
**E-commerce and retail:** Platforms like Amazon use personalization to recommend products, dynamically adjust prices and send targeted marketing messages, directly influencing sales and customer loyalty.
**Media and entertainment:** Streaming services such as *Netflix* and *Spotify* base their business model on sophisticated recommendation systems that curate movie, series and music content for users, keeping them engaged within their own ecosystem.
**Information portals and social networks:** News portals and social networks personalize the display of content (*news feed*) in order to show each user the information that the algorithm judges to be most interesting to him, thereby shaping his perception of the world.
**Education:** Adaptive learning systems (Adaptive Learning Systems*) adapt the pace and type of educational material to the individual abilities and knowledge of students, enabling a personalized educational experience (Brusilovsky & Peylo, 2003).
**Health:** In the field of digital health, personalization is used to provide individualized advice on diet and exercise, reminders to take medication, or even to identify the risk of certain diseases early based on data collected from wearable devices.

### Challenges and ethical implications
Despite its enormous advantages, the personalization paradigm faces significant challenges and ethical dilemmas. One of the most famous phenomena is the filter bubble, a term that describes the state of intellectual isolation that can occur when algorithms constantly serve the user content that confirms his existing beliefs, while simultaneously protecting him from opposing viewpoints (Pariser, 2011). Such excessive personalization can impoverish intellectual discourse and contribute to social polarization.
Furthermore, the mass collection and analysis of personal data raises complex issues related to privacy and security. How data is stored, who has access to it and for what purposes it is used is the subject of intense public and legislative debate, which has resulted in regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union. Finally, there is the constant risk of algorithmic bias, where systems, learning from historical data that reflect existing social inequalities, can perpetuate or even reinforce discrimination against certain demographic groups (O'Neil, 2016).
In conclusion, AI-powered content personalization is a powerful and ubiquitous force reshaping the digital experience. Its development poses not only technical, but also deep social, ethical and philosophical challenges. The future of these systems will depend on the ability to strike a balance between technological capabilities and human values, ensuring that personalization serves to enrich, not impoverish, the human experience.

### 7.2.5 Beyond Functionality: Beginning of Broader Considerations
Having reached the limits of the discussion about the mere functionality of technological systems, the necessity of turning towards broader and deeper considerations opens up. The questions being asked now are no longer only about *what* digital agents can do, but *how* their ubiquity changes the fundamental assumptions of human society, ethical frameworks and, ultimately, the very experience of man. With this, we leave the area of technical feasibility and enter the ground of philosophical, sociological and anthropological questioning, where the future of the coexistence of man and machine is being shaped. This step marks the beginning of a more complex reflection that will guide us in the following chapters.
