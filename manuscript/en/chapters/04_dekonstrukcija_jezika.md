# 4. Deconstruction of language in the age of AI: from symbols to social reality
The entry of large language models into everyday life in any case represents a technological breakthrough, but in many ways it also encourages a thorough rethinking of the very essence of language. The operation of artificial intelligence forces us to consider language beyond its communicative function and to understand it as a complex system that is being deconstructed and reshaped before our eyes. This process of decomposition does not lead to nothingness, but reveals the path from the traditional understanding of language as a system of symbols to its understanding as an active force that directly shapes social reality.
Classical linguistic thought, especially structuralist thought, viewed language as an ordered system of signs in which each *signifier* (word, phoneme) refers to a certain *signified* (concept, object). This connection, although arbitrary, within the language community was stable and rested on collective agreement and human experience. Linguistic models, however, destroyed that connection. They do not handle meaning in a human way; for them, language is not a network of concepts but a vast statistical space. Words are decomposed into mathematical entities, vectors, and their value is not determined according to what they mean in extra-linguistic reality, but according to the probability of occurrence in relation to other vectors within a given context. It is a mathematical deconstruction: language is stripped of its semantic core and reduced to a calculable structure.
> **Language deconstruction in the context of AI:** The process by which large language models decompose language into mathematical entities (vectors), separating it from extra-linguistic reality and semantic core; language is reduced to a computable structure of statistical patterns, thus exposing its fundamental active power independent of meaning in the human sense.
The paradox arises at the moment when these same systems, after breaking down the language into mathematical foundations, (re)construct from those foundations a text that for the human recipient has all apparent coherence, meaningfulness, and even stylistic persuasiveness. The model does not "understand" what it writes, but it produces language that *acts* as if it came from understanding. In this sense, the language model becomes an agent, an entity whose action, although devoid of consciousness and intention in the human sense, produces real effects in the world. Its output, reflecting the data on which it was trained, creates new linguistic constructs that interact with humans and other systems.
This shifts the focus of analysis from symbols to action. Language generated by artificial intelligence is not neutral. He writes programming code, drafts legal documents, creates newspaper articles, manages customer support and shapes public opinion through social media. Each of these actions represents a speech act that has a performative power - it does not describe the world, but actively changes it. The scale and speed at which these new language agents operate are unprecedented in human history.
> **Language act / speech act:** A spoken or written act that not only describes reality but also does something - a promise, an order, an obligation, the execution of a transaction; it has a performative power that changes social reality (Austin, Searle).

Our society, knowledge and mutual relations become a fabric that is woven together by human and machine linguistic agents, erasing previously clear boundaries and opening up space for completely new forms of social reality.
## 4.1 Language as a structure of meaning: symbols, relations and mental maps
Language represents the fundamental structure by which man conceives the world and himself in it. It is primarily a symbolic system, a complex weave in which every element, every word, transcends its sound or written appearance and becomes the bearer of meaning. Words as symbols stand in for things, concepts, feelings and phenomena, allowing us to mentally handle reality even when it is not immediately present. This ability to abstract is essential for the development of human thought, culture and society. Each symbol, although often arbitrary in its form (for example, there is no inherent reason why we call a tree a *tree*), acquires its permanence and communicative value through social agreement or convention (cf. de Saussure, 1916).
However, the true power of languages ​​lies in the complex web of relationships in which they are woven. The meaning derives from the position of the word within the sentence, its relationship with other words, and the grammatical and syntactic rules that govern their connection. These rules, which make up the grammar of a language, are a generative mechanism that enables the creation of an infinite number of statements from a finite number of elements. Structure is therefore what breathes life into symbols and enables precise and nuanced expression. The sentences "The agent observes the target" and "The target observes the agent" use the same symbols, but their structural difference creates two completely different meanings and describes two different realities.
This external, linguistic structure is directly mapped onto the internal, cognitive level. Through language, **mental** maps are formed in the mind of each individual - internal models of the world that serve as frames of reference for understanding, remembering, inferring and planning.
> **Mental Map:** An internal, cognitive representation of the world (concepts, relationships, experiences) that an individual builds and updates through language and experience; serves to interpret situations, make decisions and plan; it is not static, but constantly adapting.
These maps are not static photos of reality, but dynamic and adaptable constructions that are constantly updated with new information and experiences. They represent the cognitive architecture within which the agent, be it human or artificial intelligence, interprets stimuli from the environment, predicts future events and makes decisions about its own actions. Language thus becomes a tool used to build and maintain our inner world; it is the scaffolding of our knowledge (Vygotsky, 1978).

Understanding this structural dimension of language is crucial for understanding the foundations of human cognition.
### 4.1.1 Structuralist echoes in the architecture of LLMs: meaning as relation
Looking at the architecture of large language models (LLM) through the prism of linguistic structuralism, especially that of Ferdinand de Saussure, reveals astonishing conceptual similarities. Although we are talking about disciplines that are divided by an entire century and completely different scientific and technological contexts, the basic assumption about the nature of meaning as a consequence of relations within the system proves to be crucial for understanding both phenomena. The way LLMs process and generate language, mimicking human speech, essentially operationalizes Saussure's fundamental insight that meaning is differential and relational.
The founder of modern linguistics, Ferdinand de Saussure, claimed that language (French *langue*) should be understood as a system of signs in which the value of each individual element is determined solely by its relationship to all other elements (Saussure, 1916/1959). The meaning of a word does not derive from a direct and unchanging connection with a real object or concept in the world, but from its place within the linguistic structure. The value, for example, of the noun *table* is not contained in the object itself, but is established in its correlation and opposition to the nouns *chair*, *bench*, *bed* and all other components of the lexical field "furniture". In language, according to Saussure, there are only differences (French *différences*), and without positive articles. Language is therefore a form, not a substance, a pure network of relationships.
If we transfer this thought to the field of computational linguistics, we will see how large language models, although seemingly unaware of such a theoretical background, function in a strikingly similar way. The basis of their work is not semantic "understanding" in the human sense, but mathematical processing of huge amounts of textual data. Each word or sub-word (token) in the model is not represented as an isolated term, but as a vector in a multidimensional space - the so-called *embedding*. The position of that vector, and thus its "meaning" for the model, is defined solely by its geometric distance and orientation in relation to all other vectors in that space. The model learns these relationships by analyzing statistical patterns and word co-occurrence patterns in the learning corpus (Vaswani et al., 2017).
Thus, the vector space of LLM becomes a direct computer analogue of Saussure's term *langue*. What is an abstract system of oppositions and relations in structuralism becomes a concrete, mathematically defined space in the architecture of language models. The "meaning" of the token *king* to the model is not related to the historical figure of the monarch, but is determined by its vector proximity to the tokens *queen* and *throne*, its specific relation to the token *man* (similar to that between *queens* and *women*), and its distance from tokens like *apples* or *run*. The meaning is thus completely immanent to the system, it is its relational position.

Such an approach allows us to demystify the apparent "intelligence" of these systems. Their ability to generate coherent, contextually appropriate, and stylistically convincing text derives not from consciousness or intent, but from a remarkably successful replication of the dense relational web that constitutes human language. The agent, in this case LLM, does not handle concepts, but rather formal relationships between signs that it has learned from the data. Its astonishing competence is not a sign of emergent consciousness but evidence that structure itself is the carrier of what we perceive as meaning (Bender et al., 2021).
The structuralist perspective thus offers a solid theoretical framework for interpreting both the internal mechanics and the external manifestations of the operation of large language models. Although they are the product of modern engineering, their basic architecture inadvertently confirms one of the central postulates of twentieth-century linguistics: meaning within a closed semiotic system is a function of relations and differences. For LLM, "meaning" is its relational structure - it is fully summed up in a complex dance of vectors within a vast mathematical space, which reflects Saussure's thought about language as a system in which everything is difference.
### 4.1.2 The Semantic Triangle and the Cognitive Dimension: The Meaning Challenge for AI
One of the fundamental issues facing artificial intelligence is an in-depth understanding of the meaning behind the processing of linguistic data. While modern language models show an enviable ability in recognizing and generating syntactically correct sentences, the true understanding of semantic substance remains one of the biggest challenges. In order to shed light on the depth of this problem, it is necessary to look at classical models of meaning, among which the semantic triangle, also known as the meaning triangle, stands out.
This model, which was put forward by Charles Kay Ogden and Ivor Armstrong Richards (1923) in their work *The Meaning of Meaning*, offers a schematic representation of the relationships that create meaning. A triangle consists of three vertices: symbol, thought (concept or reference) and referent.
> **Semantic triangle / triangle of meaning:** Model (Ogden & Richards, 1923) that shows meaning as a relationship of three elements: symbol (linguistic expression), thought/concept (mental representation) and referent (object in the world); the connection between symbol and referent is indirect - it leads through thought; useful for understanding the limitations of AI in "anchoring" meaning.
**Symbol** represents a linguistic expression – a word, phrase or sign (for example, a sequence of letters "p-a-s").
**Thought or concept** is a mental image, concept or idea that a symbol evokes in the mind of the speaker or listener. It is the inner, abstract content that we associate with a certain symbol.
**Referent** is a real object, phenomenon or entity in extra-linguistic reality to which the symbol, through thought, refers – in our example, a concrete four-legged barking being.

![Semantički trokut značenja](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch04_semanticki_trokut.svg)
*Figure 4.4: Semantic triangle (Ogden and Richards, 1923): thought/concept is directly connected to symbol and referent, while the connection between symbol and referent is indirect and arbitrary.*
The key insight of the Ogden and Richards model lies in the nature of the connections between these three points. While the connection between symbol and thought, as well as between thought and referent, is real and causal within the cognitive system, the connection between the symbol itself and the referent is intermittent, i.e. indirect and arbitrary. The word "dog" itself has no intrinsic connection with an actual dog; this connection is established exclusively through human consciousness, that is, through the concept that mediates between the sign and what it signifies.

The concept is directly connected to the symbol and the referent, while the connection between the symbol and the referent is indirect
It is precisely in this dotted line that the fundamental challenge for artificial intelligence lies. Contemporary language models handle the apex of the triangle belonging to the **symbol** with exceptional skill. They process vast amounts of text and learn the statistical probabilities with which certain symbols appear next to each other. However, the key disadvantage arises in the establishment of connections with the other two vertices of the triangle. For artificial intelligence, the symbol "dog" is not associated with the mental concept of a dog (which includes abstract knowledge of species, behavior, biology) nor with a referent in the real world (the experience of meeting a dog, its warmth, bark or smell). Her "understanding" derives exclusively from textual correlations - words that often appear with "dog" are "barking", "bone", "leash", "faithful". AI thus creates a complex network of associations between symbols, but this network remains closed within its own digital universe, without a foothold in reality. This deficiency is called the grounding problem in cognitive linguistics.
> **Anchoring problem / grounding problem:** The challenge of connecting language symbols with extra-linguistic reality and experience; AI models learn statistical correlations among cues, but do not have the immediate access to referents or bodily experience that humans do.
Furthermore, meaning is not exhausted within the schematic relationship of symbol, concept and referent. It also has a deep **cognitive** dimension, which is inseparable from the agent using language. Human understanding of meaning is enriched by bodily experience (eng. *embodiment*), cultural heritage, emotional context and personal memories. The word "home" for man, in addition to being a symbol for the building in which he lives, evokes a feeling of security, warmth, belonging and memories related to family - layers of meaning that are built up through life experience.
Artificial intelligence, as a disembodied and ahistorical entity, is deprived of such a cognitive and experiential foundation. She does not have a body with which to experience the world, she does not belong to a culture that would shape her values, and she does not possess a personal history that would give her knowledge a unique stamp. Her knowledge is wide but shallow; it is a reflection of humanity's collective linguistic creativity, but without access to the world from which that language arose.
Therefore, the challenge of meaning for artificial intelligence cannot be solved solely with greater computing resources or more complex algorithms. It is a fundamental ontological and cognitive gap. For a system to truly understand meaning in a human way, it would have to somehow replicate at least the logical connections of the semantic triangle, if not the entire cognitive architecture that allows humans to fill symbols with living, personal, and anchored meaning. Recognizing this limit is essential for correctly evaluating the possibilities and limitations of today's language technologies.
## 4.2 Language as script: AI agents and the simulation of social interactions
The notion of language as an abstract system of signs and rules, which serves to describe the world and communicate with each other, is deeply rooted in Western thought. However, in the context of the development of advanced artificial intelligence, especially autonomous agents, language acquires a new, active role: it becomes a *scenario*, a constitutive element that shapes and moves the simulated reality. In this chapter, we explore how natural language serves as a fundamental mechanism for programming, directing, and observing complex social interactions within virtual environments populated by AI agents.
The paradigm shift from traditional programming to a language-based approach represents a key step forward. In classic systems, the behavior of an artificial entity is defined by explicit, strictly coded rules. The agent followed predetermined algorithms, and its interaction with the world was limited to a formalized set of commands and responses. In contrast, modern AI agents, powered by large language models (LLM), use language as a comprehensive instruction for action. The textual description, given in natural language, defines not only the goals of the agent but also his personality, memories, motivation and the way he perceives other participants in the simulation. Thus, the agent's ontological status is significantly changed: he ceases to be a mere executor of orders and becomes a participant in the dynamic process of creation and interpretation of social reality.
A key breakthrough in this area is research conducted at Stanford University, where a virtual city *Smallville* was created populated by twenty-five autonomous agents (Park et al., 2023). Each agent possessed a unique personality, occupation, and set of initial relationships with other residents, all of which were defined by short text passages. Agents were given no explicit instructions on how to spend their day; instead, their behavior was based on the interpretation of their own "scenario" and observations of the environment. The results were extraordinary: agents independently performed daily routines, went to work, prepared meals, but also showed emergent social behaviors. They spread information about upcoming events, gathered spontaneously, made new acquaintances and even independently organized a Valentine's Day party, without such a thing being foreseen in the initial settings.
> **Emergent behavior:** Complex behavior that arises spontaneously from the interaction of simpler rules or agents, without being explicitly programmed; in the context of AI simulation, it refers to unforeseen social patterns (cooperation, dissemination of information, organization of events) resulting from the actions of autonomous agents according to their own linguistic "scenarios".
This experiment vividly demonstrates how language functions as a backbone for three key processes in simulation:
**Creation of personality and initial states:** The initial textual description acts as the "seed" of the agent's consciousness. A passage like: "Izabela Ivanković is the owner of a local coffee shop, passionate about art and always looking for a way to bring the community together; she is on good terms with Tom, but she does not agree with his plans to build a new shopping center" not only provides information, but also establishes a complete frame of reference for future action.
**Remembering and reflection mechanism:** Agents continuously record their observations and interactions in a structure that the authors call "memory stream". It is a raw record of the event. But the key ability that distinguishes them from simple *chatbots* is the process of periodic reflection. The agent autonomously summarizes its memories and draws higher-level conclusions from them, such as "Tom often talks about his business plans" or "People in a coffee shop seem happier when music is playing." These insights become part of his permanent knowledge and influence future decisions.
**Action generation and planning:** Based on its personality, memory and deliberation, the agent makes decisions about next steps. The decision-making process involves hierarchical planning over a period of time. The agent first creates a long-term plan (e.g. "organize a meeting in a coffee shop"), then breaks it down into smaller steps (invite people, prepare the space, come up with a topic) and finally translates these steps into concrete actions (go to person X and invite them).
The entire cycle of an agent's action can be depicted as a continuous loop of perception, processing, and reaction, where language serves as the medium for each transition.


![Ciklus djelovanja AI agenta u simuliranom okruženju](../../docs/diagrams_en/diag_220.svg)
*Figure 4.5: Cyclic process of action of an AI agent in a simulated environment, where observations through memory and reflection are transformed into plans and concrete actions.*
Such an approach opens up enormous opportunities, but also poses significant challenges. On the one hand, language-driven simulations are becoming a powerful tool for the social sciences – a kind of computer laboratory for testing sociological, psychological and economic hypotheses in a controlled but realistic environment. It is possible to investigate the spread of misinformation, the dynamics of group formation or the effects of different policies on the virtual society.
On the other hand, deep epistemological and ethical questions appear. To what extent is the behavior of these agents authentic, and to what extent is it just skilful mimicry of human patterns learned from vast amounts of textual data? The line between simulation and real interaction becomes blurred, which opens up space for sophisticated deception and manipulation. Ultimately, observing agents exhibiting believable emotions, memories, and social connections forces us to question the very definitions of understanding, consciousness, and sociality. Language as a script builds believable characters and lays the foundations for worlds whose complexity and autonomy compel us to rethink our own reality.
## 4.3 Language as the architecture of society: building and maintaining social structures through communication
To understand language exclusively as a tool for the transmission of information would mean overlooking its fundamental, constitutive role in the very fabric of social reality. Language is not just a mirror that reflects existing social structures; he is an active force that builds, establishes, reshapes and maintains these structures. It is at the same time a blueprint, a building material and the very act of building a social order. In this sense, communication should be understood as a fundamental architectural process that raises and strengthens the foundations and walls of every human community.
At the core of this understanding lies the thesis about the social construction of reality (Berger & Luckmann, 1966), according to which the world we live in is not some kind of objective given but an intersubjective creation shaped by shared meanings. It is language that enables this transition from subjective experience to an objectified reality shared by all.
> **Social construction of reality:** Theory (Berger & Luckmann, 1966) according to which social reality is not an objective given, but an intersubjective creation created by common meanings, conventions and narratives; language is the key mechanism that enables the transition from subjective experience to objectified, shared reality.
Through naming, categorizing and creating narratives, language establishes a common conceptual system that allows us to experience the world in a coherent way. Concepts like *family*, *justice*, *property* or *state* do not exist as natural facts but as linguistically mediated and socially agreed institutions that then direct our behavior and shape our expectations.
Communication is therefore action, even when it only describes the state of affairs. Speech act theory (Austin, 1962; Searle, 1969) provides a key insight into this active nature of language. Each utterance, in addition to conveying a specific content (locutionary act), simultaneously performs an action (illocutionary act), such as a promise, command, question, warning or naming. When the judge says "I find you guilty", he does not describe the situation, but creates it, thereby changing the legal and social position of the accused. When two people say their marriage vows, with their words they establish a new social relationship with associated rights and obligations. Words thus become social acts with tangible consequences, and their power rests on the collective acceptance and recognition of their validity within a certain community.
It is through the repetition and mutual recognition of such speech acts that social structures such as norms, roles and hierarchies are established and strengthened. Social norms are not only written down in laws, but are confirmed and transmitted daily through communication - through approval, rebuke, advice or gossip. Social roles, such as the roles of *doctor*, *teacher* or *parent*, carry with them specific communication patterns and expectations. The way we address a person in a position of power, the language registers we use in formal and informal situations, as well as discourses that legitimize or challenge existing relationships, directly maintain and reproduce social hierarchy. Language thus becomes a mechanism by which the social order is constantly legitimized and maintained.
Within this linguistically woven architecture, an **agent** works, an individual who uses communication to achieve his goals, navigate social relationships and shape his own identity. The agent is an active participant in structures and, sometimes, a reshaper. He negotiates his position, expresses his belonging to certain groups, challenges the existing power relations or confirms them. Every communication interaction is an opportunity to confirm the existing order, but also to subtly or openly change it. Through linguistic creativity, new metaphors, changing the meaning of words or creating new narratives, agents can gradually reshape the very architecture of society.

![Kružni proces izgradnje društvene strukture kroz jezik](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch04_jezik_drustvo.svg)
*Figure 4.6: The circular process of building a social structure through language: from linguistic acts, through common meanings and norms, to institutionalization and reshaping.*
In conclusion, the relationship between language and society is strongly dialectical. Metaphorically speaking, linguistic activity is simultaneously both the building material and the builder, the blueprint and the building itself. Through communication, social structures are constantly being built, repaired and adapted to new circumstances. It shapes society, but society, with its changes, conflicts and agreements, in turn shapes and changes language itself. Understanding this unbreakable connection is essential for any deeper reflection on the nature of human community and the mechanisms of its survival and development.
## 4.4 LLMs as communicative actors: shaping the social context through algorithmic interaction
Large language models (LLMs) in the modern digital environment go beyond the role of mere tools for information processing and become active participants in communication processes. Their ability to generate linguistically complex and contextually adapted responses allows them to act as **communicative** agents, whose interaction with users directly affects the shaping of the social context. This role touches the very foundations of human communication and sociability.
The central mechanism through which LLMs achieve their social impact is to create a strong sense of **social presence**. When a user interacts with LLM, they experience communication that mimics a human conversation – the model understands queries, maintains the flow of the conversation and provides relevant responses. Interaction of this type encourages the immanent human tendency to **anthropomorphize**, i.e. attributing human characteristics, intentions and feelings to entities that do not possess them. The user therefore does not perceive the LLM as a static program, but as an interlocutor, which creates the appearance of an authentic social exchange.
> **Anthropomorphization:** Human tendency to attribute human traits, intentions, emotions and consciousness to non-human entities (animals, objects, algorithmic agents); in the context of AI interaction, the user unconsciously perceives the LLM as an interlocutor with understanding and intent, although the model only simulates a communication pattern.
This phenomenon can be interpreted in more detail through the prism of **parasocial interaction** theory, which was originally developed by Horton and Wohl (1956) to describe the audience's relationship with media personalities.
> **Parasocial interaction:** A one-sided relationship in which the viewer or listener develops a sense of closeness, friendship and intimacy with a media figure (or artificial agent), despite the fact that the figure is unaware of his existence; in the context of LLM, the user puts emotional effort into the interaction, while the model provides a simulation of reciprocity, which can lead to the illusion of two-way communication (Horton & Wohl, 1956).
In the case of LLMs, this theory proves extremely applicable. The user invests cognitive and emotional effort in the interaction, while LLM, as an algorithm, provides a simulation of reciprocity. The illusion of two-way communication is created, which can lead to the development of a strong affective bond with the artificial agent, even though it is a fundamentally asymmetric relationship.
Furthermore, the actions of LLMs as communicative actors directly shape and reshape the social context on two key levels. First, they act as mirrors and reinforcers of existing **social norms**. Being trained on vast corpora of texts derived from human culture, LLMs inevitably reproduce embedded cultural values, stereotypes, prejudices, and communication patterns in their responses. Thus, they not only reflect existing social structures, but also strengthen and legitimize them through repeated interaction with millions of users. Their responses become normative frames of reference for what is considered appropriate, correct, or acceptable.
Second, LLMs participate in the creation of new **communication practices** and expectations. Algorithmic interaction is a two-way process; users also adapt to the specifics of communication with artificial agents. They learn how to ask questions in order to get the best possible answer, adopt a certain communication style that is optimized for the machine, and get used to the instant availability and apparent omniscience of their digital interlocutor. In this way, co-shaping of communication norms occurs, where algorithmic logic gradually begins to influence human expectations of communication in general, including that with other people.
 

![Ciklus oblikovanja društvenog konteksta](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch04_llm_drustveni_kontekst.svg)
*Figure 4.7: Schematic representation of the cycle in which user interaction with LLM shapes the social context, which then feeds back on future model training data.*
However, it is crucial to recognize the nature of this new communication actor. LLM is an **algorithmic agent**, whose actions are not guided by intention, consciousness or authentic emotions, but by statistical patterns recognized in the data. His ability to engage in seemingly meaningful conversation stems from sophisticated simulation rather than true understanding. The danger lies precisely in the blurring of that boundary, where the appearance of sociability can be misinterpreted as a real social and emotional presence.
Interaction with large language models is therefore a deeply social act with far-reaching consequences that go beyond the mere exchange of information. As communication actors, LLMs actively participate in perpetuating old and creating new social norms, thereby reshaping our understanding of communication, interpersonal relationships and social reality itself. Understanding the dynamics of this algorithmic interaction therefore becomes an indispensable prerequisite for navigating a world where the boundaries between human and artificial are becoming more and more permeable.
### 4.4.1 Language as a mirror and mold of identity: the impact of AI agents on our "self"
In the complex tapestry of human existence, language emerges as the fundamental fabric that shapes and reflects our identity. It is an active force that structures our thoughts, shapes our feelings and mediates our relationship with the world and with others. Through language, our inner self – thoughts, feelings, memories and values ​​– takes tangible form and becomes visible to others, reflected in the words we choose and the ways we put them into sentences. At the same time, through language we also adopt cultural patterns, social norms and collective memories that place us within the community, thus acting as a mold that co-shapes our individuality with the collective heritage.
With the emergence and increasingly widespread use of artificially intelligent agents from simple conversational bots to advanced language models, we are entering a new period in which this fundamental connection between language and identity is facing unprecedented challenges and transformations. Namely, agents become our interlocutors, co-creators of texts and permanent mediators in digital communication. Their presence introduces a new, algorithmic dimension to the ecology of our linguistic expression, with profound implications for our understanding of our own "I".
One of the key processes takes place through subtle but steady adaptation. Constant interaction with AI agents, which are often trained on vast, but also refined and standardized linguistic corpora, can gradually and imperceptibly affect our own linguistic expression. In the pursuit of more effective communication with the algorithm, the user may unconsciously begin to adopt simpler syntactic structures, a more neutral vocabulary and a style that favors machine processing. Such a process, although seemingly harmless, can lead to the erosion of linguistic diversity and the suppression of individual, regional or subcultural linguistic peculiarities. Our "I", which is reflected in language, could thus become more uniform, adapted to an algorithmically mediated norm.
![Kružni utjecaj AI agenta na jezik i identitet](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch04_ai_jezik_identitet.svg)
*Figure 4.8: Schematic representation of the circular influence of interaction with an AI agent on language and identity: human identity is expressed through language, which in interaction with an AI agent is exposed to algorithmically shaped language, which encourages language adaptation that reshapes the initial identity.*
New linguistic adaptations penetrate to the very core of our self-concept. The language we use to describe our experiences and build personal narratives is crucial to our sense of coherence and authenticity. Sherry Turkle (2011) has already warned that technology not only changes what we do, but also who we are. In the context of AI agents, this means that they, as new and influential interlocutors, become a kind of "molds" that can subtly shape our attitudes, values ​​and even our narrative about ourselves. If the algorithm favors positive and neutral sentiments, will we gradually learn to suppress the linguistic expression of negative emotions? If it constantly offers us content that confirms our existing beliefs, will our identity become more closed and less open to change?
However, the relationship is not exclusively one-way or necessarily limiting. AI agents can also serve as platforms for linguistic experimentation and the creation of new forms of expression. They allow users to try out different stylistic personas, explore the boundaries of language, or even participate in the creation of hybrid texts where human and machine creativity intertwine. In this sense, AI acts not only as a constraining mold, but also as a sculptor's chisel that, in the hands of a skilled user, can help shape new, more layered and dynamic personal and collective identities. Understanding this dual nature - language as a mirror that can be uniformed by AI and as a mold that can be reshaped by AI - is crucial for navigating a world where the boundaries of our "self" are increasingly determined by the dialogue with the machine.
### 4.4.2 Language, artificial intelligent agents and the (re)construction of reality: algorithmic lenses and frames of meaning
Observing artificial intelligent agents exclusively as passive tools or neutral mediators of information represents a fundamental misunderstanding of their nature and operation in the modern world. Their role goes beyond simply executing orders; they are increasingly asserting themselves as active participants in the complex processes of shaping, interpreting and, ultimately, (re)constructing our social reality. The key medium through which this shaping takes place is language, understood not only as a system of signs but as the fundamental fabric of cognition and culture. Agents, operating within language systems, inevitably become active factors in the creation of meaning.
Namely, the agent does not create his image of the world through direct contact with reality, but through what we can call an **algorithmic lens**.
> **Algorithmic lens:** A metaphorical term for a complex set of computational processes, models and parameters that determine which information from the data will be selected, how it will be valued, and which will be ignored or suppressed; it acts as a filter conditioned by the architecture of the model and the corpora on which it is trained, creating frames of meaning through which the agent (re)constructs the image of the world for the user.
Algorithmically established frameworks of meaning show considerable agreement with **conceptual frameworks** which, according to **cognitive linguistics**, shape the human understanding of the world (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980).
> **Cognitive linguistics:** Branch of linguistics that investigates the relationship between language and the mind; it starts from the assumption that language is incorporated into general cognitive mechanisms and that linguistic meaning derives from conceptual structures, images, metaphors and body experience (embodiment); points out that our understanding of the world is not a direct reflection of reality, but is shaped by conceptual frameworks and linguistic categories (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980; Langacker, Talmy).
>
> **Conceptual framework:** A system of related concepts, metaphors and schemes that structure our understanding of phenomena and experiences; according to cognitive linguistics, people do not perceive the world "purely", but through such frameworks that determine what we perceive, how we categorize and how we act; frames are part of cultural and linguistic practice (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980).
However, there is an important difference. While human cognitive frameworks are the result of a complex interweaving of biological predispositions, personal experience and cultural-historical heritage, algorithmic frameworks are completely derived from statistical regularities present in data sets. As a result, every bias, every gap or predominance of certain points of view within these data is not only transmitted, but also reinforced, systematically imprinted in the very logic of the agents' actions. The (re)construction of reality offered by the agent is therefore not a reflection of reality as such, but a reflection of the world as presented in its source data.

![Proces algoritamske (re)konstrukcije stvarnosti](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch04_algoritamska_rekonstrukcija.svg)
*Figure 4.9: The process of algorithmic (re)construction of reality: from raw reality, through the algorithmic lens and frames of meaning, to the (re)constructed image of the world for the user.*
The mediation process has far-reaching consequences. When agents are used to search for information, create summaries, or conduct dialogue, they actively direct the user's attention with responses, offering them pre-framed perspectives. The reality we experience through interaction with such systems is, therefore, necessarily mediated and processed. The danger lies not only in the possibility of spreading misinformation, but in the more subtle and long-term effect of the normalization of a specific, algorithmically generated view of the world, which can suppress alternative viewpoints and impoverish the stratification of human understanding.
However, it should be emphasized that the operation of the algorithmic lens often remains non-transparent, hidden within what is called the "black box" of artificial intelligence (Bostrom, 2014). The user rarely has an insight into the reasons why some information is highlighted and others are hidden. This creates an asymmetry of power in which the agent, as an apparently objective and omniscient interlocutor, gains authority that is not based on a true understanding of the world (Searle, 1980), but on the ability to effectively process and reproduce linguistic patterns.
Ultimately, the relationship between language, artificial intelligent agents and reality opens a new chapter in thinking about the nature of knowledge and communication. The agent ceases to be a mere tool and becomes the environment, a medium through which an increasing part of our knowledge of the world is shaped. Understanding the mechanisms of these algorithmic lenses and frameworks of meaning therefore becomes not only a technical, but a first-class epistemological and social issue, crucial for the preservation of critical thinking in an age in which the boundaries between real and algorithmically mediated reality are increasingly blurred.
### 4.4.3 Language as a bridge to abstraction: creating and sharing invisible worlds
The role of language significantly goes beyond its conception as a mere tool for communication. In it lies the key to one of the most complex human abilities: abstract thinking. One of the fundamental characteristics of human language is the possibility of displaced reference, i.e. the ability to speak about objects, events and persons that are not immediately present in time and space. With language, we can recall yesterday, discuss events that took place on the other side of the world, or create detailed plans for the future.
> **Displaced reference:** The ability of language to refer to objects, events and persons that are not immediately present in time and space; the basic language power that enables abstract thinking, planning the future, telling stories about the past and creating imaginary worlds; it is considered one of the features specific to human language (Hauser, Chomsky & Fitch, 2002).
It is precisely this power of speaking about the absent that opens up the space to go one step further - to create and describe worlds that never existed outside of human consciousness. It is the source from which mythology, religious traditions, literary works and artistic ideas spring. Such an ability appears to be unique to the human species, as it has not been reported in the communication systems of other primates (Hauser, Chomsky, & Fitch, 2002).
Language therefore acts as a bridge. But it does not only bridge temporal and spatial distances, but also the gap between the inner worlds of different individuals. Using language, one agent can translate his inner, subjective experience – thought, feeling, fear or hope – into a system of common symbols and share it with another **agent**. If agent A utters the sentence "Behind that bush is a tiger I'm afraid of", he is not just conveying information about a possible danger. At the same time, he shares his inner world of fear. Agent B, having received this message, does not need to see the tiger to understand the danger and feel the echo of fear. At that moment, two agents share one invisible, but real world – the world of common cognition and emotion.

*Figure 4.10: The process by which the perception of the concrete world, through language as a symbolic bridge, is transformed into a common intersubjective reality through expression, understanding and collective acceptance.*
What is called **intersubjective** reality is built on this fundamental exchange of invisible worlds.
> **Intersubjective reality:** A network of shared beliefs, meanings and institutions (nation, law, money, corporation) that does not exist as a physical fact, but solely in the collective consciousness and narratives that people create and share through language; it has real power to shape behavior and social order (Harari, 2014).
These are networks of shared beliefs that, although they have no physical existence, have real power to shape human behavior. Concepts such as nation, rights, money or corporations exist solely within the collective consciousness and the narratives we create and share about them through language (Harari, 2014).
Without language, abstract concepts such as "justice", "freedom" or "duty" could hardly take shape, let alone become the basis of social orders. Language gives them a name, definition and content, making them the subject of discussion, agreement and collective action. It is therefore a true bridge that allows a person to step beyond the boundaries of the visible and tangible world into the expanses of abstraction, where invisible worlds are created and shared that ultimately determine human reality.
### 4.4.4 Language and Collective Creation: From Social Contracts to AI Collaboration
At the very foundations of human society and its capacity for collective action is again language. It is a constitutive mechanism that enables the creation of shared, intersubjective realities. These realities—from myths and religions to legal systems and nations—exist solely in the collective consciousness and are maintained through continuous linguistic acts (Harari, 2014). Language is therefore a prerequisite for any more complex form of human cooperation, a tissue that connects individuals into a functional whole.
This linguistic ability to create common frameworks finds its most obvious theoretical expression in the idea of ​​the social contract. Whether we understand it as a hypothetical, prehistoric act or as an ongoing process of tacit acquiescence to community norms, the social contract is at its core a linguistic phenomenon. It represents an abstract agreement on the rules of coexistence, values ​​and goals, an agreement that is established, maintained and changed through discourse. Without a language to articulate rights, obligations and expectations, such a collective agreement would be impossible. It is proof that language not only describes the world, but also actively shapes it, establishing the foundations for trust and coordinated action.
In the modern age, that long historical arc of collective creation, which stretches from the first oral traditions to complex legal systems, gets a new and unexpected participant: artificial intelligence. With the emergence of autonomous and semi-autonomous AI agents, cooperation ceases to be exclusively a human domain. These digital entities become active collaborators in processes that were once unthinkable without direct human control, from scientific research and engineering to the creative arts. Their inclusion in collective efforts represents not only technological progress but also a fundamental shift in the very nature of cooperation.

![Evolucijski put kolektivnog stvaranja](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch04_kolektivno_stvaranje.svg)
*Figure 4.11: The evolutionary path of collective creation from implicit social contracts to cooperation with artificial intelligence.*
Therefore, we face the challenge of creating a new form of "social contract" - a contract that would not be concluded between people, but between people and artificial agents. This new contract requires the development of communication bridges that go beyond simple commands and programming languages. For cooperation to be truly fruitful, it is necessary to establish some kind of common understanding and predictability. The concept of shared intentionality, developed by Michael Bratman (1987) for human teams, must be extended to mixed, human-AI teams. This implies the creation of systems in which the AI ​​agent not only performs tasks, but can also understand the wider context, the team's goals, and adjust its actions in accordance with the dynamic needs of the collaborative process.
The key element of this new contract is language, either in its natural form or through specialized formal protocols. It must enable transparency in the operation of the AI ​​agent, the ability to explain its own "decisions" and coordination with human collaborators. This confirms language not only as a tool for describing reality, but also as a fundamental mechanism of its constant creation and reshaping - a process in which our artistic collaborators have recently participated, opening up unimagined possibilities for the future of collective creativity.
### 4.4.5 AI agents as interlocutors
Viewing artificial intelligence solely as a tool for processing data or automating repetitive tasks would be too reductive. One of the most profound and transformational areas of its development is the establishment of AI agents as credible **interlocutors**. The role of an agent as an interlocutor goes beyond the function of simple chat programs (*chatbots*) that operate according to predefined scripts. This is about the emergence of complex entities capable of conducting meaningful, contextually aware and adaptive dialogue.
The basis of this capability rests on advanced models of natural language processing (NLP) and, more importantly, natural language understanding (NLU), which enable agents not only to recognize words but also to understand their context, nuances and speaker's intent. The key elements that elevate an AI agent to the level of an interlocutor are:
**Contextual Memory:** The ability to remember previous parts of a conversation and use them to inform future interactions. This avoids the feeling of talking to an entity suffering from constant amnesia, which was a feature of earlier systems.
**Coherence and relevance:** Generating responses that are logically related to the flow of the dialogue and relevant to the topic under discussion. It is therefore not only about the ability to generate grammatically correct sentences, but about the complex synthesis of information into a meaningful and appropriate reply.
**Adaptation to the user:** Advanced agents can recognize the user's communication style, knowledge level or emotional state and adjust their own tone, complexity and content of responses accordingly. Thus, the interaction becomes personalized and significantly more effective.
**Initiative in conversation:** A true interlocutor is more than a passive recipient of inquiries. Modern AI agents can ask clarifying questions, propose new topics or direct the conversation towards a productive outcome, thus becoming active participants in the dialogic process.
This evolution of interaction with technology is changing the paradigm of the relationship between man and machine. The machine ceases to be reduced to an executor of orders and ontologically rises to the level of collaborator, mentor, source of information or even partner in the creative process. The applications are far-reaching: from education, where the agent can act as a personalized tutor, through psychological support, where it offers space for non-judgmental conversation, to complex professional environments, where it helps experts analyze data and make decisions through natural dialogue.

![Evolucija interakcije čovjek-stroj](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch04_evolucija_interakcije.svg)
*Figure 4.12: The evolution of interaction progresses from one-way instruction to the tool, through two-way dialogue, to the collaborative creation of new insights with the agent.*
But this new paradigm of interaction, where the agent becomes a true interlocutor, did not arise *ex nihilo*. It is a direct consequence of the exponential growth of basic technological power and the development of increasingly complex algorithmic architectures. The ability to hold a coherent conversation, remember context, and generate human-like text requires enormous computing power to train and run language models. Reaching the AI ​​horizon, the point at which such interactions will become ubiquitous and almost indistinguishable from human ones, raises fundamental questions about the resources that make it possible. It is this drive, the invisible engine behind the facade of an eloquent digital interlocutor, that will be the subject of consideration in the next chapter.
