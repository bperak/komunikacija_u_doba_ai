## 3.1 From sign to vector: A paradigmatic shift in the representation of meaning
Observing the development of computer language processing necessarily leads us to observe a thorough transformation in the very theoretical origins of this skill. Classical approaches, which formed the backbone of Natural Language Processing (NLP) for decades, viewed language primarily as a system of arbitrary signs organized according to complex but comprehensible rules. Their starting point was deeply rooted in structural linguistics, and their goal was to impose a formal linguistic order on raw, unstructured text.

This procedure, which can be called a standard pipeline (*standard pipeline*), consisted of a series of carefully elaborated steps, each of which aims at further normalization and breakdown of the linguistic structure (Jurafsky & Martin, 2023). The first step was regularly tokenization, a process by which an unbroken string of characters is broken down into basic units – *tokens* – which most often correspond to words and punctuation marks. This was followed by lemmatization, a complex process of reducing a single word to its canonical, dictionary form or *lemma*. Thus, for example, the forms *running*, *running*, *running* would be reduced to their common lemma *running*. This put an end to the morphological diversity that hides the fundamental lexical relations. Furthermore, part-of-speech tagging was applied, where each token would be associated with its grammatical identity: *noun*, *verb*, *adjective* and so on. In many applications, such as thematic modeling, the removal of so-called stop words (*stop words*) - frequent but semantically weak words like conjunctions and prepositions - was carried out.

> **Token:** The smallest unit of text processed by the language model; can match a whole word, a part of a word (subword) or a punctuation mark. Tokenization converts raw text into a string of tokens according to a model dictionary.


The outcome of such analysis was a highly structured, but also an impoverished presentation of the text. It was reduced to a list of lemmas with associated grammatical markers, the so-called "bag of words" (*bag of words*), which broke the original syntactic structure, and thus a large part of the contextual meaning. Although these procedures were indispensable for the development of early applications, such as spell checkers or simple search systems, their essential limitation became more and more obvious: they could not effectively capture and numerically represent the very semantic core of language – a complex and fluid network of meaning relationships.

A true paradigmatic shift occurred with the appearance of **distributional semantics** and its computer application in the form of **vector representations of words**, better known as **word embeddings**. The theoretical origin of this approach is summarized in the famous thought of linguist John Rupert Firth (1957) that the nature of a word is manifested in the society it forms (*"You shall know a word by the company it keeps"*). Consequently, words are no longer seen as isolated symbols, but their meaning is constituted through the context in which they appear.


> **Embedding / investment vector:** Numerical (vector) representation of a word, sentence or other language segment in a multidimensional space; the position of the vectors reflects semantic similarity – words with similar meanings have close vectors. It is the basis of distributional semantics and internal representations in LLMs.
> **Corpus:** A collection of textual (or other linguistic) data on which the language model is learned; includes books, articles, websites and other sources. The quality and representativeness of the corpus directly affects the performance and biases of the model.

Computational models such as **Word2Vec** (Mikolov et al., 2013) and **GloVe** (Pennington et al., 2014) operationalized that insight. Analyzing huge text corpora, these algorithms learned to associate each word with a unique numerical vector in a dense, multidimensional space. The position of each vector is not arbitrary, but is determined by its relationship to all other vectors. Words that appear in similar contexts, such as *cat* and *dog*, will have vectors that are close in that space. The word *car*, which occurs in completely different contexts, will be represented by a vector that is distant from them.

Moreover, relationships between vectors can include more complex semantic analogies. For example, the vector operation *VECTOR("king") - VECTOR("man") + VECTOR("woman")* results in a vector that is in the immediate vicinity of *VECTOR("queen")*. This shows that the model has not only learned mere similarity, but also the fundamental relations that structure our conceptual world.

The transition from symbolic to distributional, vector representation of language was therefore crucial. He enabled computers to represent meaning, that most elusive component of language, in a way that is mathematically tractable and robust. Instead of relying on predefined vocabularies and rigid grammar rules, models could now learn semantic relationships directly from language usage. This opens the way for solving significantly more complex tasks, from a nuanced sentiment analysis to the creation of coherent and contextually appropriate text. Vector representations of words have thus become an indispensable cornerstone on which the entire architecture of contemporary large linguistic models, and thus also the communication agents that are the subject of this book, is built.

## 3.2 Meaning as space: From static vectors to dynamic context
The importance of the transition to vector representation of language cannot be emphasized enough. This cognitive turn made it possible to translate the abstract and fluid concept of meaning into a mathematically comprehensible form. Language ceased to be just a series of discrete symbols and became a continuous, multidimensional semantic space, in which each word is located, and its location is defined by its relationship to all others. The ability to measure distance and direction between vectors, most often through cosine similarity, turned the linguistic question of relatedness into a geometric problem. This operationalized the very essence of linguistic intuition.

However, that early vector approach also had its Achilles heel: **staticity**. Namely, each word had a single, unchanging vector, regardless of the context in which it was found. Thus, for example, the ambiguous word *hair* had the same vector in the sentences "The girl had long, blond hair" and "The peasant sharpened his hair". The model had no way of distinguishing whether it was a scalp or an agricultural tool. This limitation represented a fundamental obstacle to a true understanding of the context, which is crucial for any more complex language task.

The solution came with the revolutionary **transformer architecture** (Vaswani et al., 2017) and its central **attention mechanism**. Instead of assigning a static vector to each word, the transformer creates a dynamic, contextualized representation for each word within a sentence. The attention mechanism allows the model, while processing one word, to dynamically weigh the importance of all other words in the sentence and build its specific, contextual meaning on that basis. In our example, the model, processing the word *hair*, would pay special attention to the words *girl* and *blonde* in the first sentence, and to the words *peasant* and *sharpened* in the second. Consequently, the vector for the word *hair* would be completely different in the two cases, faithfully reflecting its meaning in the given context.

The architecture of the transformer did not only bring that qualitative leap in language understanding. Its exceptional suitability for parallel processing on specialized circuits, such as graphics processing units (GPUs), has opened the door to unimagined **scaling**. Researchers have observed that model performance predictably improves with exponential increases in three key components: model size (number of parameters), amount of training data, and computing power invested in training (Kaplan et al., 2020). This insight, known as "scaling laws" (*scaling laws*), stimulated the race to create ever larger models.

> **Transformer:** A neural network architecture based on a (self-attention) mechanism that associates each word in a sequence with a contextualized image based on the other words; it enables parallel processing and scaling to large language models (Vaswani et al., 2017).

This is how we came to the modern **large language models (LLM)**. They are not just enlarged versions of their predecessors. At a certain size level, these models began to show **emergent abilities – new skills they were not directly trained for, such as the ability to solve logic problems, write computer code, or translate between languages they had not seen during training (Wei et al., 2022).

> **Emergent capabilities:** Capabilities of large language models that appear at a certain scale (number of parameters, amount of data) and for which the model was not directly trained - e.g. inference, translation or writing code; describe a qualitative jump in behavior with increasing model size.

A large language model, therefore, is not just a language processing system. It is, in its essence, a complex model of the world, implicitly encoded in the statistical relationships of the vast corpus of human knowledge. The path from the sign to the vector, and then from the static to the contextualized vector, was therefore a path from mere formal analysis to the creation of active models of reality.

## 3.3 Entering the age of large language models
The year 2020 marked a turning point in the history of artificial intelligence with the publication of a paper on the *GPT*-3 model (Brown et al., 2020), which ushered in the era of Large Language Models (LLM). With its previously unrecorded size of 175 billion parameters, GPT-3 was a quantitative breakthrough, but also a pioneer of a new paradigm of language model operation. The key innovation was the demonstration of the ability that we now call *in-context learning*. It is about the extraordinary ability of the model to, without additional fine-tuning for a specific purpose, perform complex tasks based on only a few examples or instructions provided within the user query itself. In doing so, he demonstrated a level of generality and adaptability that far exceeded the capabilities of his predecessors.
Although *GPT*-3 laid the technical and conceptual foundations, the real resonance among the general public came at the end of 2022 with the appearance of the *ChatGPT* model. It reached a hundred million users with astonishing speed, becoming the most rapidly adopted technological innovation in history. Its success did not lie solely in the raw power of the model, which was based on the *GPT*-3.5 architecture, but primarily in the application of precise tuning using the method of reinforcement learning based on human feedback (*Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback* - RLHF). Such an approach enabled the model to follow user instructions significantly better, conduct meaningful and context-aware conversations, and avoid inappropriate or harmful responses.

> **RLHF:** Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback – an alignment methodology in which human evaluators rank the model's responses, after which the reward model is trained and the model is further tuned to maximize the expected reward; key to aligning the behavior of LLMs with human preferences (usefulness, truthfulness, security).

Accelerated development has brought us to the point where these systems cease to be purely passive text generators. Their role is being transformed, and they themselves are becoming active participants in the digital environment - agents. This transformation implies the ability of models to not only understand and generate language, but also to make decisions, plan complex sequences of actions and use external tools – from simple calculators and search engines to complex programming interfaces (APIs). We are entering a period in which large language models become proactive systems capable of autonomous action, which represents a fundamental change in our understanding and application of artificial intelligence.

## 3.4 LLM Anatomy: Key Technologies and Architectures
Understanding modern large language models (LLM) begins with an insight into the transformational architecture. Namely, the publication of the aforementioned scientific article *Attention Is All You Need* (Vaswani et al., 2017) laid the cornerstone for a new era of natural language processing, abandoning the previously prevalent recurrent (RNN) and convolutional (CNN) neural networks. The transformer architecture offered solutions to the key shortcomings of previous models, such as the problem of vanishing gradients and the impossibility of effective parallelization, thus paving the way for the development of significantly larger and more capable systems.
The central mechanism that gives transformers their exceptional power is self-attention. It is a procedure by which the model, processing a sequence of data, dynamically evaluates the importance of each word in relation to all other words within the same sequence. In this way, the model creates a rich, contextualized representation of each word, taking into account its most important semantic and syntactic connections with the rest of the text. This ability was further deepened and expanded by the introduction of multi-head attention. This approach allows the model to simultaneously, in parallel "heads", direct attention to different aspects and relationships within the sentence - one "head" can follow the grammatical structure, another thematic connections, and the third anaphoric relationships, which together creates a multidimensional and comprehensive understanding of the input text.
Since the transformer architecture does not process data sequentially, as was the case with recurrent networks, but looks at them as a whole, the inherent information about word order is lost. This shortcoming was overcome by the introduction of positional encodings (*positional encodings*), vectors that are added to the input word vectors to provide the model with information about their relative or absolute position in the sequence. In addition to the attention mechanism, a key part of each transformer block is the feed-forward network, which additionally processes the output from the attention layer and contributes to non-linear data transformation.

Based on these building blocks, three basic architectural patterns of large language models have been developed, each adapted to a specific type of task:
**Encoder-based architectures only:** These models, the most prominent representative of which is *BERT* (*Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers*), are designed for deep understanding of context. The encoder processes the entire input text at once, allowing each word to call attention to all other words in the text (bidirectional attention). Because of this ability, they are extremely effective in tasks that require text analysis and classification, such as sentiment analysis, recognition of named entities or answering questions where the answer is contained in the attached text.
**Architectures based solely on the decoder:** Models specialized for text generation, processing the sequence of words from left to right. Each new word they generate depends on previously generated words, which mimics the natural flow of human speech or writing. Their attention is causal (*causal*), that is, they can rely only on the previous words in the sequence, and not on the future ones. The most famous representatives of this group are the models from the *GPT* series (*Generative Pre-trained Transformer*), which excel in tasks such as writing essays, creative writing, summarizing and conducting conversations.
**Encoder-decoder architectures:** Also known as *sequence-to-sequence* models, these architectures combine the strengths of both approaches. The encoder first creates a complete, contextualized representation of the input sequence, and the decoder then generates an output sequence based on this representation. This pattern is ideal for transformation tasks, such as machine translation, where a sentence from one language is turned into a sentence in another, or text compression, where a longer document is turned into a shorter one. Models like *T5* (*Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer*) and *BART* (*Bidirectional and Auto-Regressive Transformers*) are examples of this hybrid structure.

#### Training process (base model, fine-tuning, alignment)
The development of large language models depends on the architecture, but also on a specific, two-stage training process. In the first phase, the pre-training phase, the model is exposed to huge, unstructured text corpora from the Internet, books and other sources. The goal is for the model to learn the basic patterns of the language independently, without human supervision (in the so-called self-supervised way): grammar, syntax, semantics and general knowledge about the world. This is most often achieved by the task of predicting the masked or following words in the sentence.
After pre-training, there is a phase of fine-tuning, where the general model is adjusted to solve specific tasks. This step may involve supervised learning on smaller, labeled data sets (eg, question-answer pairs) or more advanced techniques such as human feedback-based support learning (*RLHF*), where the model is further tuned to generate answers that are useful, truthful, and harmless, consistent with human preferences.

> **Fine tuning / fine-tuning:** The phase of adjusting the already pre-trained (basic) model for specific tasks or domains; uses smaller, labeled data and may include RLHF; the result is a specialized model (e.g. chatbot, code assistant).

#### From model to agent: a step towards action
The term agent in the context of large language models signifies a qualitative step forward: the transition from passive text generation to active action in a digital or physical environment. An agent is a system that uses *LLM* as its central cognitive drive to reason, plan, and execute tasks. Such a system has the ability to decompose complex goals into simpler steps, use external tools (such as search engines, calculators or programming interfaces), store information in memory, and learn from one's own mistakes. The anatomy of the agent thus goes beyond the internal structure of the language model and includes loops for perception, planning and action, which opens the way to the creation of more autonomous and capable artificial intelligence systems.

## 3.5 Model life cycle: from data to application
The life cycle of an artificial intelligence model includes a layered and structured sequence of steps leading from the initial idea, based on data, to the final application in a real environment. It is a dynamic and iterative process that requires the cooperation of experts of various profiles, from data scientists to software engineers. Each phase of the cycle builds on the previous one and lays the foundation for the next, thus forming a whole that ensures that the final solution is robust, reliable and purposeful. The entire process, from idea to implementation and maintenance, is in modern practice unified under the name MLOps (*Machine Learning Operations*), a term that draws its theoretical and practical roots from the *DevOps* paradigm in software development, adapting its principles to the specific challenges of machine learning.

> **MLOps:** Machine Learning Operations – a set of practices and tools for managing the life cycle of models from development to production; includes model and data versioning, automated training, deployment and performance monitoring; created by adapting DevOps principles to the domain of machine learning.

The fundamental purpose of *MLOps* is to bridge the gap between model development, which is often experimental in nature, and its reliable operational application. The process starts with understanding the business problem and defining the goals, followed by a series of technical phases that can be summarized in several key units.

#### Stages of the model life cycle
**1. Collection and preparation of data**: The starting point and fundamental prerequisite of the entire process is the definition and collection of data. The quality and relevance of the data directly determine the upper limit of the performance of the future model. This phase includes activities such as collecting raw data from various sources, cleaning them to remove errors and inconsistencies, and transforming and engineering features (*feature engineering*). The latter is particularly important because properly shaping the input variables can significantly improve the model's ability to spot relevant patterns. At this stage, techniques such as normalization, scaling and coding of categorical variables are often applied to prepare the data for machine learning algorithms.
**2. Building and training the model**: Only on the basis of the data set prepared in this way, the building of the model is approached. This step involves choosing an appropriate algorithm or model architecture, depending on the nature of the problem (e.g. classification, regression, clustering). This is followed by a training process, during which the model is exposed to data in order to learn to map input features into desired output values. During training, the model parameters are iteratively adjusted with the aim of minimizing the error function. In this phase, hyperparameters are also adjusted, i.e. external configuration settings of the model, which are not learned directly from the data, but significantly affect its performance (Bergstra & Bengio, 2012).
**3. Model evaluation**: After the model has been trained, there is a phase of its careful evaluation. The purpose of the evaluation is to objectively assess the performance of the model on data that it did not see during training. This verifies its ability to generalize, that is, its applicability to new, unknown cases. Various metrics are used, such as accuracy, precision, response or F1-measure for classification problems, or mean squared error for regression problems. It is crucial to avoid overfitting, a condition in which the model remembers the training data too well, but loses the ability to generalize.
**4. Application and integration of the model (*deployment*)**: The model that has met the evaluation criteria is ready for application in the production environment. This step represents the transition from the development to the operational world and is often one of the most complex parts of the cycle. The model can be integrated into existing software systems in different ways: as an API interface that provides real-time predictions, as part of periodic (*batch*) data processing, or as an embedded *agent* within an application or device. At this stage, the collaboration of data scientists and engineers is essential to ensure the scalability, stability and security of the solution.
**5. Monitoring and maintenance**: The life cycle of a model does not end with its implementation. Once the model is in production, it is necessary to constantly monitor its performance and behavior. The world is changing, and with it the data on the basis of which the model makes decisions. Phenomena such as concept drift (*concept drift*), where the relationship between input and output variables changes, or data drift (*data drift*), where the distribution of input data changes, can significantly impair the accuracy of the model over time (Gama, Žliobaitė, Bifet, Peciukonis, & Bifet, 2014). Therefore, monitoring systems must be in place to detect performance degradation in time and signal the need for retraining or complete replacement of the model. This closes the circle and the process returns to the initial stages, thus ensuring the long-term relevance and value of the implemented solution.
Creating a successful AI model is a marathon, not a sprint. It is a continuous cyclical process of learning, adapting and improving, deeply rooted in the data from which it originates and the application it serves.

### 3.5.1 Pre-training: creation of basic knowledge
The process of creating modern language models begins with the key and computationally most demanding phase known as pre-training. The purpose of this stage is to build what we call a foundation model (*foundation model*), which has a comprehensive and general understanding of the language, its structures, semantic relations and, to a certain extent, knowledge about the world that is embedded in the textual data on which it is trained.

> **Foundation model:** A large pre-trained model (languages, images or multimodal) that has a general understanding and can be fine-tuned for different tasks; represents the output of the pre-training phase and the starting point for specialization (fine-tuning, RLHF).
> **Pre-training:** The training phase of the language model on huge, unstructured text corpora without human annotations; the model learns language patterns and general knowledge by solving self-supervised tasks (e.g. predicting the next word). The result is a foundation model that is then fine-tuned for specific tasks.

At the heart of pre-training lies the principle of self-supervised learning.

> **Self-supervised learning / self-supervised learning:** A learning method in which the model does not use humanly marked data (input-output pairs), but performs the task itself from the data structure: for example, it predicts a hidden part of the input (next word, masked token) or another derived goal. The learning signal comes from the data itself. This procedure is crucial for pretraining large language models based on huge amounts of corpus data for which manual annotation would be difficult to implement.
> **Unstructured data:** Data that is not organized into a fixed scheme (tables, columns, tags); include free text, images, audio, video and mixed sources. Unlike structured data (e.g. databases), they do not have a predefined format, so it is necessary to convert them into a form suitable for computer processing. Pre-training for LLMs is based on vast amounts of unstructured text (web, books, articles).

The model is exposed to a huge amount of unstructured textual data collected from various sources such as websites, digitized books and scientific articles. Unlike supervised learning, this data has not been previously labeled or categorized by humans. Instead, the model learns on its own, solving the tasks it sets for itself based on the structure of the data. Most often, he is given a seemingly simple, but extremely complex goal: predicting the next word in a sentence, filling in deliberately omitted parts of the text, or some other similar task that forces him to learn linguistic regularities in depth. Going through billions of examples, the agent gradually builds up an internal representation of the language.
Knowledge acquired through pre-training is not explicitly stored like facts in an encyclopedia. On the contrary, it is woven into a complex network of billions of numerical parameters that make up the neural network of the model. This knowledge is latent and probabilistic; the model does not "know" that Paris is the capital of France in the way that a human does, but has learned that in a huge number of texts there is an extremely high statistical probability that the two phrases appear in a certain relationship. In this way, the model adopts grammatical structures, semantic relations between words (e.g. that "king" and "queen" are related), stylistic nuances and even rudimentary factual knowledge about the world.
The outcome of pre-training is therefore a basic model – a powerful, but still insufficiently precise tool. It is important to point out that this model, despite its extensive knowledge, is not in itself directly qualified to perform specific tasks such as translating, summarizing text or answering questions in a consistent and reliable manner. It represents only the raw material, the starting point for further processing, which is called **fine-tuning**. It is only in this next phase that the general knowledge of the basic model is directed and specialized for a specific application.

### 3.5.2 Fine tuning and alignment: fit for purpose
After an intensive pre-training phase, the resulting base model possesses an impressive breadth of linguistic knowledge and a certain amount of encyclopedic knowledge of the world, learned from vast data corpora (Bommasani et al., 2021). However, this version of the model is like an extremely well-educated, but unspecialized and potentially unpredictable entity. Its raw capabilities, while general, are not necessarily optimized for the specific tasks users want to perform, nor is its behavior necessarily aligned with human expectations of utility, truthfulness, and security. Therefore, in order to bridge the gap between the general potential of the basic model and the requirements of practical application, a further stage of adaptation is necessary, which typically consists of two complementary processes: fine-tuning for task specialization and alignment for shaping behavior.

Fine-tuning is the application of transfer learning, an extremely useful paradigm in machine learning where the knowledge gained from solving one problem (in this case, general language modeling during pre-training) is used as a starting point for solving another, related problem (Howard & Ruder, 2018). Instead of training a model from scratch for each specific task, which would be extremely inefficient considering the size of LLMs, fine-tuning starts with the parameters of an already pre-trained model. The model is then further trained, typically using standard supervised learning techniques, on a much smaller, carefully curated data set specific to the target task. A set consists of examples of inputs and desired outputs (tags) for that task – for example, a set of question-answer pairs for *chatbot* development, a collection of annotated medical texts for healthcare applications, annotated code repositories for coding assistance tools, or pairs of original sentences and their translations for machine translation. During this process, the model parameters—or at least a subset of them—are incrementally adjusted by optimizing a standard loss function (e.g., cross entropy) on a specific data set. The primary goal of this approach is to specialize the model to the specific requirements and characteristics of the target task or domain, while retaining and effectively using the rich general linguistic and factual knowledge accumulated during the pre-training phase. In this way, the need for large amounts of labeled data for each new task is significantly reduced and the development of specialized AI applications is accelerated. However, fine-tuning also carries the risk of **catastrophic forgetting** (McCloskey & Cohen, 1989), where the model loses part of the general abilities learned during pre-training during adaptation to a new task, which requires careful training strategies (e.g. low learning rates, gradual "unfreezing" of layers).

Parallel to the fine adjustment for specific tasks, another, by its nature more complex process takes place: **alignment**. Alignment does not only affect the domain of accuracy and information of the model, but primarily its behavior and conformity with human values and intentions. The goal is to form agents whose actions will be not only useful and true, but also harmless, i.e. aligned with ethical principles and social expectations.

> **Alignment:** Directing the behavior of AI systems according to human goals, values and safety principles; it includes methodologies like RLHF and RLAIF to make the model useful, true and safe in practice.

The key methodology for achieving this goal is **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback**, which as a rule consists of three basic steps.
The first step involves **supervised data collection,** where human annotators compile high-quality responses to selected queries. This creates an initial set of data to fine-tune the model to the desired style and tone, laying the foundation for further alignment.
The second, key step is **training a separate reward model** (*reward model*). In this phase, the language model generates several different answers to the same query, and human raters rank them according to quality, from best to worst. Based on these rankings, the reward model learns to predict which type of response a person would rate as more desirable.

> **Reward model:** Auxiliary model trained on human ratings of language model responses; predicts how a human rater would rate an answer; used in RLHF to direct the language model according to preferences (usefulness, truthfulness, security).

Its task is therefore not mere classification, but modeling of complex human preferences.
In the third step, the original language model is additionally adjusted by applying the **reinforcement learning** algorithm, most often by means of **proximal optimization** (*Proximal Policy Optimization*, PPO). The model generates answers, and the reward model evaluates them by assigning them a numerical "reward". The language model then iteratively adjusts its parameters to maximize expected reward, thereby directing its behavior toward generating responses that are consistent with learned human preferences (Ouyang et al., 2022).

The outcome of this layered process is an agent that possesses encyclopedic knowledge acquired through pre-training and the ability to interact that is adapted to human expectations and norms.
It should be noted that RLHF is not the only approach to alignment. Alternative methods are also being developed, such as **Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback** (RLAIF), where the role of the human evaluator is to some extent replaced by another, "constitutional" model of artificial intelligence that provides feedback, thereby potentially speeding up and scaling the process (Bai et al., 2022).

### 3.5.3 Optimization for the real world: efficiency
The very size that characterizes large language models, although crucial to their advanced capabilities, also represents their Achilles' heel in the context of practical application. As models grow to hundreds of billions or even trillions of parameters, training them and, more importantly for end users, running them at the **inference** (real-time answer generation) stage becomes extremely demanding. These processes consume huge amounts of specialized computing resources, such as **graphics processing units** (GPU) or **tensor processing units** (TPU), require significant amounts of memory (both for storing model parameters and for temporary activations during calculations) and consume large amounts of electricity, which entails not only high operational costs but also a significant environmental footprint (Strubell et al., 2019; Patterson et al., 2021; Luccioni et al., 2022). This resource intensity is a significant barrier to the widespread availability and implementation of LLMs, limiting their use to organizations with deep pockets and strong infrastructure, and making it difficult to run them on resource-constrained devices such as smartphones or edge devices. Therefore, efficiency optimization has become a critical area of research and development, aimed at reducing the computational, memory and energy requirements of LLMs without significantly sacrificing their performance, thus paving the way for their democratization and more sustainable application. Several key strategies dominate this field: parametrically efficient fine-tuning, quantization, and pruning.

One of the first efficiency challenges arises during the adaptation of pre-trained models to specific tasks. Traditional full fine-tuning involves updating *all* parameters of a pre-trained model on a new, specific data set. Although efficient in terms of achieving good performance, it creates a serious scalability problem: for each new task it is necessary to store a complete, huge copy of the fine-tuned model, which becomes very expensive in terms of storage and management if the model needs to be adapted for tens or hundreds of different tasks. In order to solve this problem, parameter-efficient fine-tuning (*Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning* - PEFT) techniques were developed. The basic idea of PEFT methods is to freeze the vast majority (or all) of the parameters of the original pre-trained model and update only a small number of additional or existing parameters during fine-tuning (Lialin et al., 2023; He et al., 2021). The number of parameters to be trained is often less than 1% of the total number of model parameters, which drastically reduces memory requirements (as only small sets of additional parameters need to be stored for each task, not the entire model) and fine-tuning computational costs. A few popular approaches include PEFT methods:

> **PEFT:** Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning* – a set of techniques (adapters, LoRA, prompt tuning) that adjust the pre-trained model by updating only a small part of the parameters; it reduces memory and computing costs and enables more specializations on the same basic model.

**Adapters**: This technique inserts small, additional neural modules, called adapters, within each layer (or some layers) of the original transformer architecture (Houlsby et al., 2019; Pfeiffer et al., 2020). Adapters usually have a bottleneck architecture – they first project the input activation into a lower-dimensional space, apply a nonlinear function, and then project it back into the original dimension. During fine-tuning, only the parameters of these small adapters are trained, while all parameters of the original LLM are frozen. This enables great modularity – only a new set of adapters is trained for each new task.
**LoRA (*Low-Rank Adaptation*)**: This technique, which has gained significant popularity in the field of adaptation of large language models, is based on the hypothesis that changes in model weights (ΔW) during adaptation to a specific task are inherently of low intrinsic rank (Hu et al., 2021). This means that the most important information about the model fit can be condensed into a subspace of significantly lower dimensionality.

> **LoRA:** Low-Rank Adaptation – PEFT method that approximates the change of model weights (ΔW) by the product of two small low-rank matrices; only those matrices are trained, while the original parameters are not changed; achieves performance close to full fine-tuning with significantly fewer parameters (Hu et al., 2021).

In the context of fine-tuning, instead of directly updating the entire weight matrix *W*, which often includes billions of parameters and whose direct modification would require exceptional computer resources and memory, LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) introduces an elegant solution: the change in weights (Δ*W*) is approximated by the product of two much smaller matrices *A* and *B*. Matrix *A* has dimensions (*d*\_in × *r*), matrix *B* dimensions (*r* × *d*\_out), where *r* (rank) is much smaller than the dimensions of the original matrix *W* (*d*\_in × *d*\_out). Then it applies:
- Δ*W* = *A* · *B* (product *A* · *B* has dimension *d*\_in × *d*\_out, equal to *W*)
- *W*\_adjusted = *W* + Δ*W*
During fine-tuning, only the parameters of the matrices *A* and *B* are trained, while the original weights *W* remain fixed. The number of parameters to be learned, *r* · (*d*\_in + *d*\_out), is drastically less than *d*\_in · *d*\_out in the original matrix, which reduces memory requirements and speeds up training. During inference, adjustment is applied by adding *W* + *A* · *B*, without additional latency.
![](../../docs/diagrams_en/ch03_lora.svg)

*Figure 3.5a: LoRA - low-rank adjustment: frozen matrix W and product A·B gives W_adjusted = W + ΔW.*

Empirical studies consistently show that LoRA achieves performance comparable, and often identical, to that obtained by full fine-tuning of the entire model, but with an incomparably smaller proportion of training parameters and significantly reduced computing resource requirements. This efficiency makes LoRA one of the most important and widely applied techniques for adapting large language and other deep models to specific tasks.

In contrast to traditional model fine-tuning, which involves modification of internal weights, **prompt tuning** and **prefix tuning* methods apply a fundamentally different approach. Instead of directly changing the parameters of the basic model, they rely on adding small sets of continuous, machine-learned vectors that are strategically inserted either at the input layer of the model (according to Lester et al., 2021) or into its hidden layers (according to Li and Liang, 2021). These learned vectors, known as soft prompts or prefixes, act as programmable instructions. Their function is to precisely direct the behavior of an unchanged, frozen pre-trained language model according to the specific requirements of the desired task. This avoids the need to change the original parameters of the model, which significantly reduces computational costs and the potential for "catastrophic forgetting" (degradation of performance on the original model tasks after fine-tuning).
Although this concept shares similarities with discrete query engineering, where queries are manually designed and selected, a significant advantage lies in automatic optimization. The optimal *soft query* is not chosen heuristically, but is found through an iterative learning process via gradient descent that more efficiently adapts the model for a wide range of further applications, without the need for extensive manual tuning.

PEFT techniques have revolutionized the way LLMs are adapted, making the process significantly faster, more memory efficient, and enabling easier management and serving of multiple different tasks using the same underlying model.

Another key strategy for optimizing efficiency, applicable both during training and, more importantly, during inference, is **quantization**. It refers to the process of reducing the numerical precision used to represent the parameters (weights) of the model and/or its internal activations during the calculation.

> **Quantization:** Reduction of numerical precision of parameters and model activation (e.g. from 32 or 16 bits to 8 or 4 bits); reduces memory footprint and can speed up inference; applications include PTQ (post-training) and QAT (during training) (Dettmers et al., 2022).

Standard models typically use 32-bit (FP32) or 16-bit (FP16 or BF16) floating-point numbers. Quantization aims to convert these values into lower precision formats, most commonly 8-bit integers (INT8), and more recently even 4-bit (INT4) or even lower formats (Dettmers et al., 2022; Frantar et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2022). The main advantages of quantization are multiple: first, it drastically reduces the **memory footprint of the model (e.g. going from FP16 to INT8 cuts the model size in half, and to INT4 reduces it by a factor of four), allowing larger models to run on memory-constrained hardware; second, lower precision operations (especially integer) can be significantly **faster on hardware that supports them (many modern processors and accelerators have specialized units to accelerate INT8 operations); third, reduced data transfer and simpler operations can lead to **less power consumption. The main challenge of quantization is to preserve the accuracy of the model, because reducing the accuracy inevitably introduces some error. There are two main paradigms: **Post-Training Quantization (PTQ), where an already pre-trained model is quantized without additional training (often using a small calibration data set to determine optimal quantization parameters), which is faster, but can lead to a larger drop in accuracy; and Quantization-Aware Training (QAT), where the effect of quantization is simulated during the fine-tuning or even pre-training process, allowing the model to adapt to lower precision and thus maintain a higher level of accuracy (Jacob et al., 2018). Thanks to advances in quantization techniques, today it is often possible to achieve significant compression (e.g. to 4 bits) with very little or almost no performance loss on downstream tasks, making quantization an extremely popular method for efficient implementation of LLMs.
The third important optimization technique is pruning, which is based on the idea that many large neural models are overparameterized, that is, they contain a significant number of parameters (weights) or even entire structural components that are redundant or do not significantly contribute to the final performance of the model. Pruning aims to identify and permanently remove these less important elements, thereby reducing model size and potentially speeding up inference (LeCun et al., 1990; Han et al., 2015).

> **Pruning:** Removal of less important parameters or entire structures (neurons, layers, attention heads) from the model to reduce size and speed up inference; it can be unstructured (single weights) or structured (whole units); it is often combined with additional fine-tuning (LeCun et al., 1990; Han et al., 2015).

We distinguish between two main types of pruning: unstructured and structured.
**Unstructured pruning** (*unstructured*) removes individual weights from the weight matrices of the model, usually based on some importance criterion (e.g. removal of weights with the smallest absolute value – *magnitude pruning*). Although it can achieve very high thinning rates (e.g. remove 90% or more of the weights) while maintaining accuracy, the resulting weight matrices become sparse with irregular patterns of zeros. Standard hardware (GPU, CPU) is often not optimized for efficient use of this structure, so the achieved reduction in the number of parameters does not necessarily lead to a significant acceleration of inference in practice, unless specialized hardware or software libraries are used.
**Structured pruning** (*structured*) removes entire, regular structural units of the model, such as individual neurons, channels in convolutional networks, or, in the context of transformers, entire attention heads (*attention heads*) or even entire layers (Li et al., 2016; Voita et al., 2019; Michel et al., 2019). Because entire blocks are removed, the resulting model is smaller but still has a dense structure that can be efficiently executed on standard hardware, often leading to real speedups in inference while reducing the number of parameters. Determining which structures to remove is usually based on an assessment of their importance to model performance.
Pruning is often performed iteratively: the model is trained, some parameters are trimmed, and then the model is further fine-tuned to recover from the potential drop in accuracy caused by pruning. An interesting link is the **Lottery Ticket Hypothesis** (Frankle & Carbin, 2019), which postulates that within large, randomly initialized neural networks there are small subnets ("winning tickets") that, when trained in isolation from the beginning with the same initialization, can achieve performance comparable to the original large network. Although finding those *slips* is not trivial, this hypothesis further supports the idea that large models are inherently redundant and that there are much smaller, more efficient architectures waiting to be discovered or extracted. Techniques such as knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), where the knowledge of a large *teacher* model is transferred to a smaller *student* model, are also often used, sometimes in combination with pruning, to create smaller and faster models (e.g. DistilBERT (Sanh et al., 2020)).
Techniques such as parametrically efficient fine-tuning, quantization and pruning, often used in combination, are key tools for bridging the gap between the theoretical capabilities of large language models and the requirements of practical applications in the real world. They make LLMs not only more feasible to implement in a wider range of scenarios, including those with limited resources, but also reduce their economic and environmental costs, contributing to their democratization and paving the way to a more sustainable AI future.

### 3.5.4 Measuring success: evaluation
Evaluation represents a key, but also a layered procedure in the development and understanding of intelligent systems, going beyond mere quantitative measurement to include a critical assessment of their purposefulness and effectiveness. In the context of artificial intelligence and autonomous agents, evaluation is an indispensable component of the entire research and development cycle. Its fundamental purpose is to provide an objective insight into the agent's performance, whereby its value is not exhausted in the mere determination of performance. On the contrary, meaningful evaluation seeks to penetrate the reasons behind a certain outcome, explaining why and under what conditions the agent acts successfully or unsuccessfully. This opens the way to targeted improvements and a deeper scientific understanding of observed phenomena.
In a theoretical and practical sense, it is common to distinguish between two fundamental levels of evaluation, which are not mutually exclusive, but complement each other. These are internal and external evaluation.
**Internal evaluation is focused on the analysis of individual components or modules within the agent itself.** Its goal is to evaluate the effectiveness of specific algorithms, models or mechanisms from which the agent was created. For example, with a natural language processing agent, internal evaluation could separately measure the accuracy of the entity recognition module or the performance of the semantic analysis algorithm. Such an approach enables precise locating of defects and optimization of individual parts of the system, regardless of its final task.
**External evaluation,** on the other hand, observes the agent as a complete, monolithic entity and measures its success in carrying out the final task intended for it within the given environment. In this approach, internal mechanisms are ignored, and attention is focused exclusively on the outcome. For example, if it is a chess-playing agent, an external evaluation would measure its winning percentage against other players or programs. With a customer service agent, the degree of customer satisfaction or the time required to resolve a query would be measured (Jones, 2021). This approach provides a final assessment of the agent's practical usability.
In order for the evaluation procedure to be scientifically based and credible, it must rest on several key principles. First of all, the evaluation must be **objective**, which implies the use of clearly defined and measurable metrics that are independent of the subjective judgment of the researcher. Furthermore, it must be **repeatable**, which means that other researchers, using the same data, settings and methodology, must be able to obtain identical or statistically comparable results. This principle guarantees the verifiability and reliability of scientific knowledge. Equally important is **reproducibility**, a broader term that implies the possibility that other teams, starting from the description of the method, independently implement the system and conduct a similar experiment and come to the same conclusions.
Ultimately, evaluation should not be understood as a static act of judgment, but as a dynamic and integral part of the development process. It forms a feedback loop that enables continuous improvement. Evaluation results, whether positive or negative, provide valuable information that guides further development, debugging, and refinement of the agent. This establishes an iterative cycle that is at the very core of progress in the field of artificial intelligence.

Iterative development cycle of the agent, where after evaluating and analyzing the results, a decision is made to improve and redevelop or end the cycle

### 3.5.5 Model in action: inference
After the artificial intelligence model has been successfully trained and evaluated, it moves from the development phase to the application phase. At that stage, its primary purpose becomes acting on new data to create value for the user or system. This process, in which the model generates predictions based on new, previously unseen input data, is called inference. It represents the pinnacle of the entire model development process, the moment when abstract knowledge, acquired during training, turns into concrete and useful answers.
Unlike the training procedure, where the model parameters are dynamically adjusted in order to better map the input data to the desired output values, during inference the model remains static. Its learned internal structure and parameters are fixed and applied to new data without further modification (see e.g. Goodfellow et al., 2016). An agent, be it a human or a software system, inputs data into the model – for example, an image for classification, a sentence for translation, or a set of sensor readings. The model then, applying its learned internal representations, processes this data and provides relevant information as an output – prediction, classification, summary or some other form of structured response.

### 3.5.6 Ways and environments of performing inference
From a technical point of view, inference can be carried out in two basic ways: batch and in real time (real-time or online).
**Batch inference implies the processing of large amounts of data at once,** in predefined cycles. This approach is suitable for applications where immediate response is not critical, such as generating daily business reports, analyzing market trends on a weekly basis, or processing archival data. The main advantage of this method is the high throughput and efficiency of processing large data sets.

> **Group inference / batch inference:** A way of executing a model in which large amounts of data are processed at once in predefined cycles; suitable when latency is not critical (reports, analysis, archive); enables high throughput and efficiency.

**Real-time inference, on the other hand, refers to the processing of individual or small groups of data as soon as they become available,** with an emphasis on minimal latency, i.e. the time required to obtain an answer. Such an approach is necessary in systems where quick reaction is essential, for example in the detection of fraud in card transactions, content recommendation systems on websites or in the management of autonomous vehicles.

> **Inference in real-time:** A way of running a model (*real-time* or *online* inference) in which individual or small data sets are processed immediately upon availability, with an emphasis on minimal latency; necessary in systems for fraud detection, recommendations, autonomous driving and similar applications.

The choice between batch and real-time inference is closely related to the system architecture and the environment in which the model is executed. Models are often placed on cloud servers, which provides scalability and access to large computing resources, but may introduce latency due to network communication. On the other hand, in order to reduce latency and ensure data privacy, models are increasingly performed on edge devices (*edge computing*), such as smartphones, industrial sensors or cars, or on local infrastructure (*on-premise*).

> **Edge computing:** Performing computer processing and models on edge devices (phones, sensors, vehicles) instead of exclusively in the central cloud; reduces latency and the need for data transfer, suitable for privacy and real-time applications.

Inference is therefore the moment when an abstract mathematical model becomes a concrete tool, capable of making decisions, automating processes and providing insights into the real world. It is a bridge between the theory of machine learning and its practical application.

## 3.6 Challenges and Limitations: Shadows in the Digital Mirror
While large language models demonstrate increasingly impressive abilities in understanding and generating language, opening the door to transformative applications in almost all spheres of human activity, their rapid development and implementation are inevitably accompanied by a series of serious challenges and inherent limitations that cast a shadow over their potential. Issues related to the security of their use, the reliability of the information they generate, and the potential for misuse of their powerful capabilities are critical obstacles that cannot be ignored.

> **Bias:** Systemic deviation in algorithm or model behavior resulting from training data – e.g. social prejudices, stereotypes or underrepresentation of certain groups; it can lead to discrimination in decisions (employment, credit, criminal proceedings) and the perpetuation of injustice (O'Neil, 2016).

One of the fundamental difficulties, rooted in the very essence of machine learning, is the issue of **algorithmic bias**. Artificial intelligence systems learn based on the data that humans provide them, and this data often reflects existing social prejudices, stereotypes and inequalities. An algorithm, as a mathematically unbiased entity, cannot by itself correct the human shortcomings woven into data sets; moreover, it can amplify and automate them, thus creating systems that systematically discriminate against certain groups of people in employment, credit approval or even in criminal proceedings (O'Neil, 2016). Thus, technology, instead of being a tool for achieving justice, turns into a tool for perpetuating and legitimizing injustice.
This is directly connected to the problem of **transparency** and **explainability**, often summed up in the term "black box".

> **Transparency (in the context of AI) (*transparency*):** Availability of information about how the system works - what data and algorithms are used, how decisions are made and who is responsible; it is a prerequisite for supervision, trust and responsibility. With complex models (e.g. deep networks), transparency is limited because the internal logic is difficult to read ("black box").
>
> **Explainability:** The ability to provide the user or supervisor with an understandable reason why the system made a certain decision or prediction; in the AI context it is often investigated through XAI (explainable AI) methods. It is crucial in areas of high risk (medicine, law, credit) where it is necessary to justify and verify the outcome of the algorithm (Pasquale, 2015).

Many advanced artificial intelligence models, such as deep neural networks, operate in a way that is extremely difficult or even impossible to fully interpret. Although the system can make an extremely precise decision or prediction, the path by which it arrived at that decision remains hidden within complex layers of mathematical operations. This lack of transparency poses serious obstacles, especially in high-risk areas such as medicine or autonomous driving. How can we trust the machine's decision if we don't understand its logic? Who is responsible when such an inexplicable system goes wrong? The inability to see into the agent's reasoning undermines trust and prevents effective control and error correction (Pasquale, 2015).
In addition to technical obstacles, the application of artificial intelligence also poses profound social and economic challenges. Automation, powered by increasingly capable intelligent agents, threatens to transform the labor market on an unprecedented level. While the previous industrial revolutions mostly replaced manual work, today's technology is increasingly encroaching on cognitive tasks that were considered exclusively human domain. The issue of massive job losses and the consequent growth of economic inequality is raised. The creation of new jobs may not keep pace with the disappearance of old ones, which may lead to a deepening of the digital divide – not only in access to technology, but also in the skills needed to navigate the new economic order.
At the center of these technological and social changes is man, whose privacy, autonomy and well-being are facing new challenges. The operation of intelligent systems is based on the collection and processing of huge amounts of data, often personal. This erases the boundaries between private and public, and the surveillance possibilities become almost unlimited. The constant monitoring of digital traces, carried out by both corporations and state apparatuses, opens the door to manipulation, from subtly influencing consumer habits to shaping political opinion within personalized information bubbles and echoes (Zuboff, 2019). Man's autonomy in decision-making is thereby threatened, and his exposure to algorithmic management is increasing.
Furthermore, it is necessary to warn about the danger of excessive reliance on technology and the gradual loss of human skills. If we leave critical thinking, memory or even creativity to machines, we risk our own cognitive atrophy. The ability of machines to generate convincing text, images and sounds also raises the question of authenticity and truth. In a world where it is increasingly difficult to distinguish the real from the fake, and misinformation spreads at the speed of light, trust in institutions and in the media space itself is deeply shaken.
All the mentioned challenges point to the fundamental need for the establishment of solid ethical frameworks and regulatory mechanisms. The development of technology far outpaces the speed at which laws are passed, creating a regulatory vacuum in which decisions about the future of humanity are left to technology companies driven by profit interests. The question of responsibility, the global nature of artificial intelligence that escapes national legislation, and the danger of its misuse for military purposes and the creation of autonomous weapons are just some of the problems that require urgent and coordinated international cooperation.
Facing the shadows in the digital mirror is not a call to reject technology but to its thoughtful and responsible development. Recognizing the challenges is the first step towards solving them – a step that requires the collaboration of experts from various fields, from the technical sciences to philosophy and law, to ensure that artificial intelligence truly serves the benefit of all humanity, not just a narrow circle of the privileged few.

### 3.6.1 Security, Reliability and Abuse
Any comprehensive reflection on the reach and application of autonomous agents inevitably touches on three key and interconnected areas: system security, its reliability in operation and the possibility of misuse. Those three elements form the basic framework for assessing the sustainability, ethics and practical usability of any agent system, because neglecting any of them can lead to serious technical, social or security consequences.

#### Security
**Security**, in the context of autonomous agents, refers to the protection of the system, its data and processes from unauthorized access, modification or destruction. It includes technical measures that ensure the integrity and confidentiality of the system, as well as its resistance to external attacks. Malicious actors may attempt to exploit vulnerabilities in program code, system architecture, or communication protocols to take control of the agent, steal sensitive data that the agent handles, or disable it.
A special challenge in the field of artificial intelligence is represented by so-called **adversary attacks**, where input data (for example, an image or text) is subtly changed in a way that is imperceptible to humans, but which leads the agent to a completely wrong conclusion or action (Goodfellow et al., 2014).

> **Adversial attack:** Deliberate alteration of input data (image, text) subtle to the human eye, but which leads the model to make a wrong classification or decision; it requires robust models and verification mechanisms beyond traditional information security (Goodfellow et al., 2014).

Protection against such attacks requires robust models and verification mechanisms that go beyond traditional information security methods. A security breach not only compromises the agent's functionality, but directly undermines user trust and opens the door to further abuse.

#### Reliability
Closely related to security is **reliability**, which means consistency and predictability in the agent's work. While security deals with protection against external threats, reliability focuses on the internal correctness and robustness of the system. A reliable agent must consistently perform its tasks in accordance with defined parameters and goals, even in unforeseen circumstances or when encountering new, unexpected data.
A system that is reliable is not fragile; it is robust to errors caused by edge cases, noise in the data, or its own internal limitations. One of the key reliability problems in modern language models is the phenomenon of "hallucinations", where the agent generates convincing, but factually incorrect or meaningless information (Ji et al., 2023). Ensuring reliability therefore implies rigorous testing, validation and establishment of mechanisms for detecting and correcting anomalies in agent behavior. An unreliable agent, even if it is perfectly safe, loses its practical value because its decisions and outcomes cannot be relied upon.

#### Abuse
Finally, the third member of that trinity is **misuse**, which refers to the intentional use of an agent for harmful, unethical or illegal purposes. Unlike security flaws that are exploited by external attackers, misuse implies that an agent is used as designed, but to achieve malicious goals. Paradoxically, the very features that make agents powerful—autonomy, the ability to learn, adapt, and lead complex interactions—also open the door to their abuse.
The possibilities of misuse are manifold: from the automated dissemination of disinformation and propaganda, through the execution of sophisticated social engineering attacks and identity theft, to the coordination of malicious activities in the digital or physical world. The agent can be used to create fake content (*deepfakes*), bypass security checks or automate processes that harm individuals or organizations. The problem of abuse goes beyond the purely technical domain and goes deep into ethical and legal issues, posing to society the challenge of establishing norms and regulations that will reduce potential damages.
Those three aspects – security, reliability and abuse – are not independent categories, but form a dynamic and connected system. Weak security facilitates abuse, while low reliability can create unforeseen security vulnerabilities. Effectively dealing with these challenges requires a holistic approach that combines technical innovations in the field of robustness and protection, with clear ethical guidelines and legal frameworks for the responsible development and application of autonomous agent technology.

### 3.6.2 Bias, Privacy and Data Ethics: The Dark Side of Digital Mirrors
Digital systems, especially those based on artificial intelligence and machine learning, are often thought of as objective, neutral mirrors that reflect the world as it is. This metaphor, although seductive, hides a deeper and more complex truth. The mirrors we create are not perfectly flat or impeccably clean; they are distorted, and the reflections they offer carry within them the shadows of bias, violated privacy and ethical doubts arising from the very foundations on which they were created - from data. Looking at the dark side of these digital mirrors is key to understanding their real impact on society.
The fundamental problem stems from the nature of the data the algorithms are fed with. Contrary to the widespread, almost mythical belief about the objectivity of the machine, algorithmic bias is not an intrinsic flaw of the code, but an inevitable reflection of the data that the algorithm is "fed". These data are not abstract entities but distilled human experience, with all its historical injustices, social inequalities and prejudices. When an algorithm learns from employment data that reflects decades of discrimination against women in certain sectors, it learns not about objective qualifications, but about historical reality. His model will, consequently, perpetuate that same discrimination, not out of malice, but out of the logic of a learned pattern. Face recognition systems that show significantly lower accuracy for darker-skinned people or women are not the result of bad programming, but rather the insufficient and unrepresentative representation of these groups in the training data sets (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018). Thus, the digital mirror not only reflects the existing social flaw, but also reinforces it, giving it the appearance of technological unquestionability and creating a dangerous vicious circle.




![](../../docs/diagrams_en/diag_200.svg)

*Figure 3.9: Schematic representation of the cycle in which biased data leads to biased models, whose decisions then reinforce and legitimize the initial social biases.*

Along with the problem of bias, the issue of privacy is equally serious. The age of big data (*Big Data*) gave birth to the economy of surveillance (*surveillance capitalism*), in which personal data is considered raw material, the most valuable resource of the new age (Zuboff, 2019). Each of our digital traces – from searches on the Internet, through location data and communication on social networks, to biometric information – becomes an object of collection, processing and monetization. The boundary between the private and public spheres is becoming increasingly permeable, and the consent given by users is often just an illusion, hidden behind impenetrable legal formulations and unexplainable user conditions. The consequences of such conditions go deeper than targeted advertising. The loss of privacy threatens the very core of personal autonomy, freedom of expression and the ability to act without fear of constant surveillance and judgment. When information about our health, political beliefs or intimate relationships becomes available to third parties - be it corporations, government bodies or malicious *agents* - the scope for manipulation, blackmail and social control on an unfathomable scale opens up.
Ultimately, bias and privacy merge into a unique and layered ethical challenge. Who is responsible for the harm caused by a biased algorithm? Is it the programmer who wrote the code, the company that implemented the system, the user who uncritically accepted his decision, or the society that created the data burdened with injustice? Traditional models of accountability, designed for human actors and predictable cause-and-effect chains, prove inadequate in a world where autonomous *agents* make decisions with far-reaching consequences. This requires us to develop new ethical frameworks that will be able to encompass the complexity of the digital ecosystem. A radical paradigm shift is needed: from reactive error correction to proactive, ethically based design (Ethics by Design). This implies transparency in the operation of algorithms, the right to explain automated decisions, and the establishment of independent bodies for supervision and audit.
Digital mirrors are therefore not passive observers, but active participants in reshaping our reality, often in ways that are subtle but profoundly influential. And the challenge before us is not to break those mirrors, but to learn how to build them with wisdom and maintain them with a sense of responsibility, aware that the reflection we see in them is not only a picture of the world as it is, but also a blueprint of the world as it could become, through our actions or inaction.

### 3.6.3 Resource intensity: the price of intelligence
Any manifestation of intelligence, whether biological or artificially created, carries with it an inevitable cost. In the field of artificial intelligence, especially in the field of deep neural networks and large language models, this cost takes on enormous proportions, manifesting itself not only in financial expenses, but also in the consumption of material and energy resources. The drive towards increasingly advanced and capable agents has created a paradigm in which the complexity and performance of models is directly related to the amount of computing resources invested. This **resource intensity** has therefore become a key feature of the modern development of artificial intelligence, raising fundamental questions about its sustainability, availability and environmental consequences.

> **Resource intensity:** Demanding development and application of AI models in terms of computing power, memory, energy and financial costs; especially pronounced for large language models whose training and inference require massive GPU/TPU clusters, which limits availability and has environmental consequences.

The fundamental driver of this exceptional computational hunger is the scaling paradigm, an empirically confirmed law according to which model performance improves at a predictable rate with an increase in the number of parameters, the size of the learning data set, and the amount of computing power invested in the learning process (Kaplan et al., 2020).

> **Scaling laws:** An empirical insight according to which the performance of language models increases predictably with the increase of three factors: the number of model parameters, the amount of learning data and the amount of computing power; it encouraged a race to build increasingly large models (Kaplan et al., 2020).

The scaling paradigm stimulated a kind of race in the construction of increasingly large models, whose architectures contain hundreds of billions, even trillions, of parameters. Learning such colossal structures requires time-consuming and complex computations on specialized circuits, such as graphics processing units (GPUs) and tensor processing units (TPUs), organized into massive clusters that operate for weeks or even months non-stop.

> **TPU:** Tensor Processing Unit – a chip designed to accelerate machine learning operations, especially matrix calculations in deep networks; optimized for high throughput and energy efficiency in training and inference of large models.

The costs of such an undertaking reach tens and sometimes hundreds of millions of dollars for just one learning cycle, including the cost of hardware, electricity and maintenance.
A direct consequence of this enormous energy consumption is a significant ecological footprint. The process of learning large artificial intelligence models is one of the most energy-intensive computing tasks. Studies that have sought to quantify this impact have yielded troubling results. For example, the learning process of a large language model with architecture search is estimated to have produced an amount of carbon dioxide that is comparable to five average car life cycles, including their production (Strubell et al., 2019). This realization puts technological progress in direct conflict with global efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, imposing an ethical dilemma on researchers and companies operating in the field.

The increase in the size of models and data leads to an exponential growth of computing requirements, resulting in high financial costs, high energy consumption and obstacles to democratization and a significant ecological footprint.
Furthermore, the high cost of computing resources creates a deep gap and poses serious obstacles to the democratization of technology. The development of the most advanced models becomes the exclusive domain of a small number of technological giants and national laboratories that possess sufficient capital and infrastructure. Academic institutions, independent researchers and smaller companies are finding it increasingly difficult to keep up, leading to the consolidation of power and influence in the hands of a few.

> **Computing gap:** Asymmetry in access to computing resources (GPU/TPU, energy, capital) needed to develop and run large AI models; it limits which actors can participate in development, leading to consolidation of power and biases in technology.

Such a "computing gap" limits the diversity of ideas, slows scientific review and replication of results, and opens up space for biases that reflect the values and interests of those who can afford to develop such systems.
It is important to distinguish between training costs and inference costs.

> **Learning costs and inference costs:** Learning costs (*training costs*) – one-time, but extremely high costs of training the model (hardware, energy, time). Inference costs – continuous operational costs of applying the already learned model to each query; at billions of queries per day cumulatively they can be huge.

While learning is a one-time but extremely intensive process, inference refers to the continuous use of an already learned model to perform specific tasks. Although the resource consumption for individual inference is significantly less than for learning, the cumulative cost can be enormous when the model is applied to millions or billions of queries per day, as is the case with popular digital assistants or translation services. Therefore, the total cost of intelligence should include the investments necessary for its creation, as well as the permanent operating cost of its existence and operation. Addressing this multi-layered challenge requires a shift towards the development of more energy-efficient algorithms, optimized hardware solutions, and more transparent reporting of the real cost of progress in the field of artificial intelligence.

## 3.7 Transformation of communication: applications and perspectives
The emergence and wider application of artificial intelligence, especially large language models, brings radical changes to the very core of human communication, reshaping its fundamental paradigms and opening up unimagined possibilities, but also posing new challenges. We are witnessing a period in which technology ceases to be perceived exclusively as a passive channel or tool and takes on the characteristics of an active participant and even co-creator of communication processes. This transformation fundamentally changes the way individuals, organizations and entire societies create, exchange and interpret information.
One of the most prominent applications is manifested in the possibility of creating hyper-personalized content, where communication messages are tailored according to the specific needs, interests and previous behavior of an individual user. In the field of marketing and advertising, this enables the delivery of relevant offers that significantly increase consumer engagement, going beyond traditional, mass approaches (Chen et al., 2021). Similar principles are applied in the media, where algorithms suggest news and articles according to the reader's habits, creating a unique information experience for each individual.
These principles of personalization also extend to the area of customer support, which is undergoing a thorough transformation. Once limited and scripted *chatbots* are giving way to advanced conversational agents capable of conducting complex dialogues, understanding context and providing solutions to a wide range of user queries in real time. Such systems not only increase efficiency and reduce operating costs but also improve customer satisfaction by providing immediate and constantly available support (Kumar et al., 2022).
Furthermore, artificial intelligence is becoming an unavoidable factor in the content creation process itself. Large language models are used today to compose marketing texts, news reports, social media posts, and even to write programming code or creative literary works. The role of the human creator thus changes - he increasingly becomes an editor, curator or collaborator who directs and refines the content generated by the machine. Such a collaborative relationship opens up enormous creative possibilities, but at the same time raises complex questions about authorship, originality and credibility of information (Liang et al., 2023).
Viewed in a long-term perspective, the development of autonomous agents represents perhaps the most profound change on the horizon. These sophisticated software entities, powered by artificial intelligence, are designed to act proactively on behalf of their users, performing complex tasks that require planning and interaction with other systems and people. An agent could, for example, independently organize a business trip, negotiate the terms of a contract or manage the user's digital identity. In such a scenario, communication no longer takes place only between people or between man and machine, but also between autonomous agents representing human interests.

Finally, it should be pointed out that this technological revolution carries with it considerable ethical and social implications. The possibility of generating convincing but **false information** (*deepfakes*) represents a serious threat to social trust and democratic processes. The automation of communication roles raises questions about the future of workplaces, while the ubiquitous mediation of technology prompts discussions about alienation and the loss of authenticity in interpersonal relationships (Brynjolfsson and McAfee, 2018). Therefore, in parallel with technological progress, it is crucial to develop strong ethical frameworks and monitoring mechanisms that will ensure that the transformation of communication serves the well-being of humanity, and not its undermining. The future of communication will inevitably be shaped by the interplay of human ingenuity and artificial intelligence, and the outcome of that interplay will depend on the wisdom with which we manage its development.
